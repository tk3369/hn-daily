Source: neil.fraser.name · HN Discuss · 1993 pts / 489 comments

The .name TLD is being shut down, and owners are furious

The registry plans to terminate the entire .name TLD, wiping out existing third-level registrations rather than sunsetting gracefully. Commenters call it a broken promise — the sane path would be freezing new signups while honoring existing owners.

What's happening

  • All existing .name third-level registrations face termination, not just a freeze on new signups.

Owner grievance

  • Sole third-level holders can't buy the corresponding second-level domain even when they're the only registrant — registrars have refused for 15+ years.

Practical risk

  • Released second-level names become squatting targets, and owners have no path to keep their identity.

What HN commenters are saying: The consensus is that discontinuing new registrations while honoring existing ones (and reserving the underlying 2LDs against squatters) would be the decent move; terminating existing registrations is seen as bad faith. Several owners report asking registrars like VeriSign for the second-level name they alone use and being refused for over a decade. A side thread debates bulk domain ownership, with a Fortune-50 anecdote explaining why companies hold hundreds of domains rather than wildcards.

Source: inference-docs.cerebras.ai · HN Discuss · 614 pts / 204 comments

Qwen 3.8 27B hits 1500 tokens/s on Cerebras

Qwen 3.8 27B is now served on Cerebras at a claimed 1500 tokens per second — extremely fast per-request, but the public endpoint's 150k input-tokens-per-minute cap throttles real coding workflows. The consensus: burst speed is exciting, sustained throughput is what matters.

What shipped

  • Qwen 3.8 27B available on Cerebras inference at roughly 1500 tokens/s.

The catch

  • 150k TPM input limit means about three 50k requests per minute — a few seconds of Cerebras-speed usage before cooling down.

Workarounds debated

  • Multiple accounts (loses KV-cache continuity), dedicated capacity, or just running it locally on a 16GB card like a 5060.

What HN commenters are saying: Users who tried Cerebras's earlier coding plan report the same frustration — blistering per-request speed undermined by rate-limit pulses, with sessions only about 10% faster on wall clock. There's a technical debate about why KV-cache resumption doesn't solve it, framed as an 'Amdahl's law of inference economics' where SRAM and shoreline bandwidth are the contended resources. A pragmatic minority says skip the subscription: a 27B model runs on a consumer GPU, slower but far cheaper.

The largest electric aircraft just flew — with a catch

The largest electric aircraft has flown, but commenters quickly established it's a reserve-hybrid design, not pure electric. The hybrid backup exists mainly for certification and diversion safety, with electric-first operation for the first roughly 125 miles of a 500-mile range.

What flew

  • The biggest electric (primarily) aircraft, demonstrated on video.

The correction

  • It carries a reserve-hybrid configuration burning aviation fuel — the title implies pure electric, reality is electric-first with a fuel reserve.

Why hybrid

  • Diversion scenarios can require landing hundreds of miles away, and regulators won't certify without a backup powerplant.

What HN commenters are saying: The top thread is a correction — the founder mentions 'hybrid' around the 1:30 mark, and the fuel reserve is confirmed in the video. Most commenters are fine with the tradeoff: electric motors have far lower failure rates than combustion engines run near redline, and using batteries for takeoff/climb while reserving fuel for cruise or emergencies improves the safety margin.

Go champion Shin Jinseo beats KataGo — with the AI getting a two-stone handicap

Human Go grandmaster Shin Jinseo defeated the AI KataGo while the AI received a two-stone handicap — a notable result given KataGo's strength. The thread spends as much time on rating-system math as on the game itself.

The result

  • Shin Jinseo (strongest human, ~3850+ rating) beat KataGo with a two-stone handicap to the AI.

Context on dominance

  • Shin is about 120 rating points above his nearest human rival — a larger gap than Magnus ever held in chess; Ke Jie peaked at 3755.

Rating discussion

  • Go and chess both use Elo-like systems, but Go's dynamic range is far wider, so raw numbers aren't directly comparable.

What HN commenters are saying: Several readers initially misread the headline (thinking the AI gave the handicap). The substantive discussion is rating theory: GoRatings uses an Elo variation, EGF adopted an Elo-inspired system in 2021, AGA uses a Bayesian system — the key point being that only rating differences within one system are meaningful. Shin's roughly 120-point gap over the next human is historically unprecedented.

Source: techdirt.com · HN Discuss · 332 pts / 134 comments

Hackers had a live feed of every ID scan one verification company processed — for a year

Hackers reportedly maintained a live feed of every ID verification scan a company processed for over a year. The thread turns into a design debate: document-scan identity verification is broken by design, and the fix is cryptographic — per-service certificates instead of raw document images.

What happened

  • A live stream of every ID scan through one verification vendor was exposed to hackers for more than a year.

Root cause framing

  • Centralized storage of raw ID images is the flaw; one breach compromises everything.

Proposed fix

  • PKI-based attestation — the issuer provides certificates, users hand out single-use per-service credentials, and no raw ID data sits in a vendor database.

What HN commenters are saying: The dominant view is that chain-of-trust PKI (like the EU's age-verification approach, where the issuer never learns who you showed the cert to) is the correct architecture, and several European eID systems prove it works. Skeptics counter that PKI implementation quality in the wild is poor — 'I don't trust anyone to get PKI right' — and that even correct systems get fooled by social workarounds like borrowing an older friend's verified code.
