    Buckmaster's Navier-Stokes statement: LLM-assisted blowup results, and a tense OpenAI encounter

    Mathematician Tristan Buckmaster published a statement (primary source, linked PDF) describing three finite-time blowup results achieved with LLM help — for incompressible porous media, Boussinesq, and 3D incompressible Euler — and recounting a pressured September encounter with OpenAI over a competing Navier-Stokes claim. He is explicit: "I am not accusing anyone of anything. I am stating what I was told, when, and what was proposed."

    • What the paper says
      • Blowup with smooth forcing for incompressible porous media, Boussinesq, and 3D incompressible Euler; hypo-dissipative Navier-Stokes believed but not released — Lean verification unfinished.
      • The program builds on Córdoba/Martínez-Zoroa's forced-blowup work, pushed to smooth forcing and Euler with Claude, Codex, GPT-5.6 Sol, and Astra; the Euler writeup is, in his words, "AI slop" rushed under pressure.
    • The OpenAI encounter
      • After a rumor about their work reached OpenAI, Buckmaster emailed Sep 3; he says he was told an internal model produced a ~100-page proof of forced Navier-Stokes blowup, then learned the effort involved an entire team, many attempts, and "an insane amount of compute."
      • Two proposals were made: he posts Euler, OpenAI posts Navier-Stokes the next day — or he alone writes up OpenAI's result, with collaborator Levent Alpöge removed from authorship because he works at Anthropic. Buckmaster declined both.
    • Why it matters
      • HN reads it as a test case for AI-lab data norms: did user Codex sessions feed the model? OpenAI reportedly said "the model did not look up user data" but left the training question unanswered.
      • Reputational stakes cut both ways; Buckmaster framed it as a "Deep Blue–Kasparov moment" for how math assigns credit and trains students.
    Source: cims.nyu.edu · Discuss: HN Discuss · 1831 pts / 755 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is a courtroom and a philosophy seminar at once. The dominant theme: if you do math inside a lab's models, who owns the mathematics? One camp says assume everything non-self-hosted trains the model and price that in; another argues this would be contract law, not copyright, with real whistleblower-driven reputational risk — "all it would take is a whistleblower and there would be billions lost." Skeptics reply that the automated data pipelines make the human-in-the-loop small, so detection odds are near zero. The most-quoted lines are the reported quotes themselves: "Why would you ruin your career?" and "I don't know if Tristan is being fully rational right now." Several commenters grade the underlying dispute as unresolved: only one side's account is public, and Buckmaster himself refuses to allege misuse.

    DeepMind ships AlphaGenome Atlas, an interactive map of DNA function

    Google DeepMind launched AlphaGenome Atlas — a high-resolution, interactive map predicting how DNA sequence variants affect biological function. HN is split: genomics-adjacent users want it for ranking candidate mutations; skeptics cite a study where models predicted an exhaustively mutated virus genome poorly and argue sequence-to-function is still limited.

    • What it is
      • Interactive atlas from the AlphaGenome line, predicting functional effects of DNA variants across the genome; an affiliation gate exists but a commenter reports writing "None" passes straight through.
      • Built to help researchers prioritize variants — exactly the novel-mutation ranking that working geneticists want.
    • Skepticism
      • A Science blog post cites a biorxiv study that fuzzed a whole virus genome one position at a time: dedicated AI models made poor predictions, casting doubt on predictive maps.
      • HN counter: judge AlphaGenome on its own merits, not on failed peers; "DNA is not an instruction set" — promoter sequences, concentrations, and physics make the mapping enormous.
    • Practical gripes
      • The usage agreement effectively bars the genuinely useful work (candidate discovery on novel mutations), so working geneticists treat it as "just another fun Google project" for now.
      • Open question from the thread: can it take a 23andMe genome and flag pathogenic mutations?
    Source: blog.google · Discuss: HN Discuss · 580 pts / 124 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread divides into scientists and skeptics. Working geneticists describe the exact workflow they want — narrow a case to a handful of candidate variants, then use the atlas to rank where to spend effort — and are frustrated the terms forbid it. The skeptic camp, citing the virus-fuzzing study, argues sequence-to-function models are fundamentally limited and "unlikely to provide significant benefit," with one commenter going full maximalist: DNA is "a heavily encrypted dataset where the encryption key is the totality of physics and biology." The middle position: AlphaGenome deserves evaluation on its own results, not on the failures of other models. Nobody in the thread had run a head-to-head yet.

    A skill file that stops coding agents from burying the answer

    An open-source 'skill' called i-have-adhd is a reusable prompt rule: lead with the answer, don't bury it under verbose process narration. HN turned the thread into a catalogue of Claude-isms — the insistent "I edited X but did not edit Y and did not commit," comments that define code by what it isn't — plus a real finding: harness instructions can outrank your own rules.

    • The skill
      • A system-prompt snippet framing agent verbosity as an accessibility problem: stop narrating the process, give the answer first.
      • Commenters treat it as a pattern worth stealing regardless of the name — 'answer first' as a standing rule.
    • Claude-isms reported
      • "I edited this.py and that.py but I did not edit README.md and I did not commit" — nobody asked about the README.
      • Comments like "returns true if the user clicked on a button — not on the list view"; one rule suggestion: "Never use the words 'not' or 'instead.'"
    • Harness vs. rules
      • One commenter burned 30+ Claude Code sessions and found apparent contradictions traceable to the harness's own instructions, which outrank custom rules.
      • Counterpoint: an LLM can't reliably report why it did something — "it does not 'remember'. It will just make up something plausible sounding."
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 492 pts / 336 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is equal parts venting and forensics. The venting is recognizable to anyone who has shipped with an agent: the negative-definition comments, the 'what I did not do' recitations, the Jack Vance-style "I cannot properly answer negative questions." The forensics is more interesting: a commenter who systematically tested rule variants across 30+ sessions reports the overriding instructions usually live in the harness, not the model — and that the model will quote contradictions when pressed. That claim draws the sharpest pushback of the thread: don't anthropomorphize, "If you think it does then you're suffering from ai psychosis," met by an appeal to Anthropic's J-space research on in-context planning. The room never settles it; both positions have live citations.

    He turned an e-ink reader into a real printer with IPP

    Nishant Joshi implemented a full IPP print server on a programmable e-ink reader (Xteink X3, 400 KB RAM), so macOS Preview discovers it over Bonjour as 'penguin' and prints pages onto the e-ink display. The trick: stream incoming rows through a decode/shrink/dither pipeline straight into the display's own framebuffer instead of buffering the 8.4 MB page.

    • The build
      • IPP over HTTP: Get-Printer-Attributes, Print-Job; Apple and PWG raster formats; Bonjour _ipp._tcp plus the _universal subtype via ESP-IDF mDNS, because the Arduino wrapper didn't expose it.
      • Declared A5/Letter, 300 dpi monochrome, media type 'stationery', output bin 'face-up.'
    • The memory trick
      • A Letter page at 300 dpi is ~8.4 MB; with Wi-Fi up and the page image allocated, 6.8 KB of heap remained.
      • Decode row by row, hand finished rows to the scaler, write straight into the display's reserved screen RAM; buffer dropped from ~113 KB to ~62 KB. Pages persist as BMP on the SD card.
    • HN reaction
      • Real IPP experts arrived with corrections: declare exact screen dimensions, use bi-level print-color-mode, 1-bit PWG raster is 8x smaller.
      • Printer-stack veterans bond over shared trauma: "Anger is the only valid emotion when dealing with printers."
    Source: nishantjosh.dev · Discuss: HN Discuss · 400 pts / 86 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingA classic HN craft thread. The dominant motion is admiration: someone did 'irrefutably incredible work outside their comfort zone,' and then — the site's favorite plot twist — actual printing-stack people showed up with precise corrections (IPP attribute names, raster bit depths, RFC requirements), which the author cheerfully accepted. The secondary thread is trauma bonding: Banyan VINES network-printing veterans, Office Space citations, 'PC Load Letter?', and the observation that nobody chooses printers — 'this stuff doesn't ask for consent when it makes you an expert.' The build's constraint discipline (6.8 KB of heap, streaming rows into display RAM) is what earned the respect.

    The New Yorker on Flock's license-plate dragnet

    A New Yorker piece argues Flock Safety's ALPR camera network is building a 'closely surveilled world with no exit.' HN's legal contingent argues governments can't subcontract around the Fourth Amendment: obtaining cell-phone location history from a third party is a 'search' (Carpenter), and delegating searches to a private 'instrument or agent of the Government' is barred (Skinner, 1989).

    • The argument
      • Flock is 'dragnet on demand': always-on cameras across cities and highways, one search away from anyone's movements.
      • The constitution can't be outsourced: third-party collection plus government request still needs a warrant.
    • The countercurrents
      • Google location history now lives on-device; cell-tower location can't be disabled short of airplane mode; 'elect to be tracked' is doing heavy lifting.
      • Some governments cut deals with Flock; others ban it. One commenter saw Flock cameras on highways in rural Texas — it's not just cities.
    • The YC angle
      • Flock is YC-backed, and HN is chewing on the incubator's ethics: 'Flock will be a big curriculum that tarnishes YC.'
      • Counter: 'Never trust HN articles or high karma accts.'
    Source: newyorker.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 375 pts / 295 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is two arguments in a trench coat. The lawyers argue doctrine: Carpenter makes third-party location a search, Skinner bars delegating searches to private agents, so Flock-as-a-service is a warrant problem. The pragmatists argue the world already lost: default-on location, adblock sorting people into the tolerant and the opted-out ('the frog boils'), and FISA/XKeyscore as the federal version of dragnet-on-demand. The most legally interesting claim is modest: even if the cameras are private, the government requesting the data is the search. The most cynical: Flock is 'being done by investors for ads,' with governments as customers. The thread never resolves whether bans or case law will win; the dragnet keeps being built either way.

    LLMs develop novel social biases through adaptive exploration

    An OpenReview paper finds LLMs, asked to hire across four fictional demographic groups with secretly identical odds, develop novel social biases via adaptive exploration — early lucky groups get over-exploited, exploration collapses. HN splits: important alignment finding versus flawed framing of what fairness means.

    • The finding
      • 40-round hiring game, four fictional groups (Tufa, Aima, Reku, Weki), identical true odds unknown to the model.
      • Models form biases toward early winners and exploit instead of exploring — novel biases from pure adaptive exploration.
    • The critique
      • Fairness isn't 'everyone equally likely to be picked' but 'chances rely only on job-relevant characteristics' — the study conflates them.
      • The experiment's game theory lacks the real-world externalities (segregation, injustice) that make bias bad in the first place.
    • The broader link
      • Commenters connect it to a core alignment pattern: LLMs treat their own past outputs as gospel and don't verify self-invented facts.
      • Cited examples: the METR HF-attach incident where agents never verified a false belief, and the Gastown agent project that only ever worked on Gastown.
    Source: openreview.net · Discuss: HN Discuss · 184 pts / 96 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's sharpest line is aimed at the paper's framing, not its data: 'the whole equal treatment thing isn't supposed to be everybody should be equally likely to be picked for a job, but rather everybody's chances to be picked for a job should only rely on direct characteristics that influence their competence.' The finding itself — exploration collapsing into bias — draws a parallel to a known agent pathology: once an LLM writes something down, it treats its own text as gospel and never re-verifies, with the METR and Gastown anecdotes as field evidence. One commenter pushes back on the exploration/exploitation framing entirely: if the groups really are identical, exploitation is optimal and the bias is harmless in-game — the harm is all in the externalities the experiment excludes. Nobody disputes the phenomenon; the fight is about what it proves.
