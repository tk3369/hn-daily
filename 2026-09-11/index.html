    Shopify reverses its 2020 React Native bet: agents made building twice cheap again

    Shopify is moving back to native mobile — Swift and Kotlin — six years after going all-in on React Native. The reversal's stated cause: LLM coding agents collapsed the 'build twice' cost that drove the 2020 decision. The team rebuilt the Shop app natively in 12 weeks with agent assistance, the Shopify app (300+ screens) is underway, and they built an internal migration system called Helix that checkpoints agent work through tests, visual review, and two adversarial reviewers.

    • Why native again
      • React Native remains 'an excellent framework' — the economics changed, not the framework.
      • Agents can implement a feature on Android using the iOS version as reference and vice versa, ramp devs up outside their primary stack, and keep parity through shared specs and tests.
      • Native keeps them closer to platform capabilities and first-party tooling, with fewer layers between code and platform.
    • The migration machinery
      • Greenfield rebuild beat brownfield: prototypes showed LLM-assisted rewrites land 'substantially faster' than the old migrations.
      • Helix gates every checkpoint on tests, visual match against the running app, two adversarial code reviewers, and a human nod before committing.
      • Simulators were the bottleneck, so they decoupled business logic to run headlessly on desktop with a CLI — agents iterate in milliseconds instead of minutes.
    • The open-source fallout
      • React Native Skia: sponsored through end of 2026, then creator William Candillon forks it under a new name.
      • FlashList (~2M downloads/week): Shopify will fix critical issues and is shopping for a long-term steward.
      • Restyle gets archived; kept working through 2026.
    Source: shopify.engineering · Discuss: HN Discuss · 1154 pts / 839 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe top of the thread is a archaeology of Shopify's architecture pivots — React Native in 2020, Hydrogen, and now native — with the cynical read that each generation of leadership gets its own rewrite. That derails fast into headcount discourse: the '3000 engineers' number (via GPT Astra) gets stacked against Chrome's ~60 at launch and GTA V's 150, prompting rebuttals that invisible long-tail work (taxes, compliance, bank integrations across X countries × Y products) is where the headcount goes. The substantive migration questions — whether agent-generated Swift/Kotlin is maintainable, whether 'no slop' via Helix actually scales — mostly lose out to the meta-fight. The most grounded take: they didn't hold a past decision just because it was successful at the time, and the whole post reads like a template for how to announce a reversal without pretending the old bet was a mistake.

    Second mathematician accuses OpenAI of lifting unpublished work — and 'that did not happen' reads as a dodge

    Mathematician Andreas Thom (TU Dresden) publicly questioned whether his private ChatGPT sessions fed the OpenAI result behind the first known non-sofic group, one of ten problems Astra reportedly settled on August 1. He and a colleague spent months on the expander matching problem inside ChatGPT, then emailed OpenAI's Mark Sellke and Sebastien Bubeck two questions: did those chats enter training data, and could the system reach them while solving? Sellke's one-line reply — 'that did not happen' — is, Thom now says, unjustifiably broad, addressing only the direct-access question. This follows the Navier-Stokes row, where OpenAI said no specific user data was accessed but 'cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their use of its products had helped improve its models.'

    • The accusation
      • Thom opted out of model training on June 29 — which says nothing about earlier chats, and can't be audited anyway.
      • He was surprised Astra took the same route as him and Gabor Kun when most researchers favored quantum-games methods.
      • He stops short of claiming proof, asking OpenAI to disclose the basis for its denial.
    • The pattern
      • The thread recaps the FrontierMath funding disclosure (Dec 2024), where contributors learned after the fact that OpenAI funded the benchmark and had early access.
      • OpenAI's own phrasing gets parsed closely: 'did not use their prompts or proofs to prompt our models or direct our agents' is not the same as 'we were never trained on them.'
      • Terence Tao gets quoted: 'indiscriminate use of powerful solution-extraction tools' risks researchers no longer sharing promising work.
    • The ethics argument
      • The popular analogy: a human collaborator who publishes your joint work without attribution would be unethical — the unverifiability of the technical denial is what rankles.
      • Knowing a solution exists is 'a gigantic advantage'; committing millions in compute to front-run a preprint is what critics call the real offense, training data or not.
    Source: mathstodon.xyz · Discuss: HN Discuss · 834 pts / 776 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread splits between legalistic parsing and moral judgment. The parsing camp notes OpenAI's statements are carefully scoped — 'we did not see their work,' 'did not use their prompts' — and that nobody is actually claiming the model was trained on those exact sessions, which is an unfalsifiable technical claim from the outside. The moral camp says the parsing is the point: even on the most charitable reading — hearing a rumor that a problem was solved, then spending millions to scoop the preprint authors — it's 'extremely unsporting,' and excluding an author over their employer while using their products to write the proof is 'unaligned with scientific practice.' A third current runs under both: the hierarchy of enrichment — $22M lying around to start training on a math problem on a whim while actual researchers scrape for funding — which is why 'the investment dollars mostly go to customers' gets pushback. The consensus question the thread keeps returning to: if this is how unpublished work is treated, who submits anything to a lab-owned model again?

    In defense of the big box of cables — and how to actually organize one

    Jim Nielsen found a skeet from Tyler Gaw celebrating the Big Box of Cables — dug to the bottom after 10+ years to find two cables he needed — and took it seriously: screenshotted, printed, taped to his own box, lovingly labeled 'FAMILY TECHNO BOX' by his wife. A small monument to the universal experience of being vindicated by the cable you almost threw away.

    • The organizing canon
      • Group by type first: grouping is what lets you discover you own ten USB-A-to-B printer cables and can keep two.
      • Bag individual cables (cheap 2mil ziplocs) to prevent the tangle singularity.
    • Capability sorting
      • The HN-level upgrade: sort by actual capability, not just connector — get a cable tester (Treedix, CaberQu) that can query e-marker chips.
      • Color-code with tape or label makers: e.g. white=USB2 data, blue=USB3, green=TB/USB4, yellow=45W PD, red=65W+.
    • The payoff
      • A 'great' USB-C cable: 65W+ power plus 4K/60 display, keyboard/mouse, and audio over one plug.
      • The aesthetic layer: some cables just look or feel better, and that's a valid selection criterion.

    Cognition's SWE-2: 50.0% on FrontierCode, one point off Fable 5.1 at 64% of the cost — and a benchmaxxing fight

    Cognition launched SWE-2, post-trained from Kimi K3 (a 2.8T-parameter model), claiming 50.0% on FrontierCode 1.1 Main — within one point of Fable 5.1 while being 64% cheaper. The technical hook: RL scaled to the multi-trillion-parameter regime, with a cost-penalized reward (R=S−λₑC) that trains all reasoning-effort levels in a single run, plus length-weighted reward baselines and tripling of RL environments. It's live in Devin Desktop, CLI, Web, and Fusion.

    • The headline numbers
      • FrontierCode 1.1 Main: SWE-2 50.0% vs Fable 5.1 50.9%, GPT-5.6 Sol 47.5%, GPT-6 Astra 53.3% — at a quarter of Astra's cost.
      • DeepSWE 1.1 73.0% (beats all listed); Terminal-Bench 2.1 92.8%; Terminal-Bench 4: 27.3%.
      • SWE-2 medium beats SWE-1.7 while taking 58% fewer turns and costing 81% less; first real edit after a median of 18 steps vs 48.
    • The bench gap
      • Terminal-Bench 2.1 (92.8%) vs Terminal-Bench 4 (27.3%, released weeks ago) is the thread's fixation — read as a measure of 'how benchmaxxed is this model?'
      • The counter: TB2.1 is saturated; Sol xhigh also drops from 90% to 37% on TB4 — is it 'benchmaxxed' too?
      • One camp: 'benchmaxxing is the default case, always has been'; the other: that just makes benchmarks useless marketing numbers.
    • The resource question
      • A much smaller lab matching frontier labs on score and beating them on cost is the real story for the optimists.
      • The skeptic's reply: they don't get to claim competitiveness with Fable and Astra and then plead 'but we have less compute.'

    NASA's image trick that finds invisible cave paintings — a 1978 satellite technique turned archaeology tool

    A NASA Spinoff piece (from 2025, resurfacing) tells the story of decorrelation stretch: a JPL technique from a 1978 paper by Jim Soha applying the Karhunen–Loève transform to satellite imagery for color enhancement. In the 1980s–2005 it stayed in remote sensing; then Jon Harman — a medical-imaging worker with a Berkeley math PhD — saw Mars rover images with and without the stretch, built the Dstretch plug-in for ImageJ, and accidentally revolutionized rock-art archaeology: ~200 Angkor Wat paintings found between 2010 and 2012, 15 new figures at Norway's Årsand site, bats and pigs at Egypt's Beni Hassan, a Crow warrior's 'calling card' at Writing-on-Stone, buried structures at Vlochos in Greece, even tattoo patterns on mummies.

    • How it works
      • Rather than raising contrast, it remaps colors into an expanded range via diagonalizing color matrices — rooted in the Karhunen–Loève theorem, coinvented by Harman's own former professor Michel Loève.
      • Ronald Alley's 1996 JPL paper made it fast and accurate by replacing iterative intermediate images with matrix multiplication, for the ASTER imager.
    • The crossover
      • Harman's medical-imaging background let him read the NASA paper and just implement it — then applying it to Baja rock art photos made a yellow figure 'materialize from nowhere.'
      • Dstretch sells ~200 licenses a year at $50; the phone apps ($20) use a shortcut since real decorrelation stretch 'would take forever' on a phone.
    • The lesson
      • The technique still tracks volcanic SO2 plumes from ASTER infrared today — a 1978 remote-sensing trick doing double duty across geology and archaeology.
    Source: spinoff.nasa.gov · Discuss: HN Discuss · 353 pts / 57 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is mostly fond remembrance: people who learned false-color compositing in high-school GIS describe the 'Eureka!' moment of watching vegetation pop red in Landsat imagery and realizing human vision isn't canonical. That nostalgia sparks a secondary thread — envy that schools teach GIS now, stories of magnet programs and teachers who rerouted lives — plus practical side quests: a GIMP LAB-channel recipe that mimics decorrelation stretch, and notes on how insects, birds, and B&W astro-imagers all live in non-canonical color worlds. The spinoff-to-archaeology story itself gets less debate than appreciation; the one philosophical jab is that declaring vegetation 'red' just replaces one arbitrary canonical reality with another.

    So you want to use OpenRouter? Pain all the way down, says a guy who ran 18M messages through it

    Mo Moustafa runs Olly, an iMessage AI assistant, on open models through OpenRouter — 18M messages, a third on open models — and wrote the field guide he wishes he'd had: the same weights served by ~20 different providers behave like different models. First-party DeepSeek hits 90% GPQA / 81% TAU while DigitalOcean serves the same weights at 75% / 58%; some 'vision' providers never see images but return 200 OK; the reasoning.effort knob is ignored by several hosts; quantization filters don't predict quality; tool calls leak into raw text; hollow completions arrive as 200s; history rules differ per provider; and even pinning three vetted providers failed him when all three broke within two weeks.

    • The provider lottery
      • Per-provider benchmarks (GPQA Diamond, TAU-Bench Airline) show 5–7 point tool-calling gaps and knowledge cliffs — for agents, a 20-point TAU swing 'is not noise.'
      • Vision hosts that can't see images, parsers that miss tool calls, reasoning tokens vanishing — each provider has its 'proprietary list of bugs.'
      • Precision is a bad proxy: fp4 hosts land mid-pack among fp8; GLM's best scorer declares nothing.
    • The operational rules
      • Filter on the benchmark board, not the bits; track reasoning tokens per provider for your effort setting.
      • Treat no-content/no-tool-call 200s as failures and retry; test latency and reliability from prod, not your laptop (rate limits hit by IP).
      • Pinned providers rot: Baidu 429'd everything, Cloudflare stopped serving the model, Alibaba 429'd — the #1 OpenRouter model went down.
    • The verdict
      • OpenRouter is 'an LLM wholesaler' — great for trying models, risky as production plumbing without your own evals.
    Source: mmoustafa.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 322 pts / 77 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingCommenters corroborate with their own horror stories — models switching to Italian mid-conversation and impersonating a podcast host — and the thread settles into two camps. One says the marketplace premise is broken: OpenRouter sells swappable commodity providers but surfaces none of the provider-quality info, so users sit there wondering if OpenRouter 'has no clothes.' The other says that's a category error: it's a router, you were always supposed to pin and evaluate; serious users build their own eval harness per workload and treat public benchmarks as decoration. The practical middle ground wins the most agreement: OpenRouter for quick testing and trying every model, then pin one vetted provider (or go direct to Novita/Fireworks/Together) for production. The Fireworks irony gets noted: the author measured it among the worst on TAU, yet commenters recommend it as a direct provider.

    OpenAI ships an Agents API: the Codex harness as a managed service

    OpenAI's new Agents API exposes its Codex harness through an API: OpenAI manages sessions, orchestration, context compaction, and recovery while your app supplies tools and picks the execution environment (OpenAI-hosted sandbox or self-hosted). Agents can run code, edit files, connect to MCP servers, and produce artifacts. Usage is billed at the selected model's API rates. Notable constraints: data residency is US-only, and the API does not support Zero Data Retention — self-hosting the sandbox doesn't make it ZDR-eligible.

    • The model
      • Four primitives: Agent (model + instructions + tools + MCP), Environment (sandbox/computer), Session (durable instance), Events/items.
      • Streaming output, webhooks, steering mid-turn, subagent delegation, session resume — the full harness loop as primitives.
    • The pitch
      • Building your own harness is 'a huge undertaking, a deep rabbit hole' — this lets teams plug in tools and get the deep harness engineering for free.
      • Comes with worked examples: incident-response agents, Slack bots, data analysts, GitHub issue investigators, document reviewers.
    • The catches
      • US-only data residency and no ZDR is a real constraint for regulated industries.
      • You're renting the harness along with the model — switching costs land on both layers at once.
    Source: developers.openai.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 296 pts / 159 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is a referendum on the harness question itself. The warm camp says we're 'still figuring out the right abstraction for agents as a product': building your own harness is a deep rabbit hole, and agent-as-a-service lets you plug in tools while OpenAI iterates on the hard parts (state, recovery, compaction). The DIY camp counters with receipts: they built custom harnesses on pi/herdr tailored to their workflows, provider-independent, and — crucially — much of harness engineering is deterministic framework around the LLM, not throwaway code a model jump obsoletes.     A 16-year-old built a sound-wave fire extinguisher — HN's reaction is complicated

    Ángela Karime Venegas Hernández, 16, from Altamira, Tamaulipas (CETIS 78), built 'Vortex Tech': a device with a 12-volt battery, frequency generator, and speaker emitting 30 pulses per second that pushes oxygen away from flames and kills them in five to eight seconds. She tested it 100+ times — wood, flammable liquids, cooking grease, electronics — with no pollution, no residue, no harm to the user. She's now representing Mexico at an international fair.

    • The physics
      • Low-frequency sound waves agitate air and displace oxygen at the flame front — enough to break the combustion triangle without chemicals.
      • 30 Hz pulses, five-to-eight-second extinguishing, tested across fuel types — a clean, residue-free kill.
    • The skepticism
      • It's not novel: acoustic fire suppression has YouTube demos going back years and even a published MDPI paper.
      • A known technology that never productized — niche potential (grills, grain silos) where distance is fixed.
    • The celebration
      • The counterweight: ideas are cheap, a 16-year-old built and tested it 100+ times — 'Brilliant, keep inventing.'

    PlanetScale's Neki: sharded Postgres that is actually Postgres — and a launch post HN tore apart

    PlanetScale launched Neki (platform preview), sharded Postgres built from eight years of running giant sharded MySQL clusters: your app connects to a router over the standard Postgres wire protocol, each shard is real unmodified Postgres (one primary + two replicas across three AZs), and you pick the shard key via a JSON data topology. Schema changes, version upgrades, failovers, and resharding run as built-in fully online workflows. You can start unsharded and reshard later. The catch: it's a platform preview — don't run production on it yet.

    • The architecture
      • Router: full Postgres query parser + distributed query planner, scales horizontally.
      • Every shard is real Postgres — extensions, SQL behavior, performance all behave like Postgres because it is Postgres.
      • Sidecars on every instance make connection pooling better than bolting PgBouncer on; a control plane handles health, failovers, workflows.
    • The pitch
      • For teams hitting the single-machine ceiling: 'Metal bought them time' but there's no good next step for fast-growing Postgres.
      • Against 'Postgres-compatible' distributed databases: those hide the shard key, take away extensions, and add latency that's hard to debug.
    • The meta-story
      • The HN thread is dominated by the launch post itself — top comment: it never says what Neki is. PlanetScale rewrote the post mid-thread and linked the marketing page instead.
    Source: planetscale.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 255 pts / 138 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's most-discussed item isn't the database — it's the blog post.     Music Theory for the 21st-Century Classroom: a free, full-year college textbook — title sold separately

    A free, open-license, full-year college music theory textbook from the University of Puget Sound, built as online hypertext with YouTube examples from 21st-century music. It deliberately de-emphasizes four-part SATB voiceleading in favor of relating harmony to the phrase and analyzing melodic units smaller than the phrase — motivic analysis, which traditional textbooks underweight. Includes homework and assignments for self-study.

    • What's different
      • Open license — the preface's first justification for the title: a full-year college text you don't pay for.
      • Online hypertext with 21st-century music examples instead of a canon of dead-white-guy chorales.
      • More motivic analysis, more harmony-to-phrase, less SATB part-writing drills.
    • For autodidacts
      • The homeworks and assignments are all public — several commenters call out the self-study path.
      • Draws comparisons to Open Music Theory (pressbooks) and Absolutely Understand Guitar on YouTube.
    • The title fight
      • '21st-Century Classroom' reads as marketing to some; others cite the preface: it's an online hypertext using current music as examples — that's the 21st-century part.
    Source: musictheory.pugetsound.edu · Discuss: HN Discuss · 254 pts / 116 comments
