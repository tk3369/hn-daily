    A misalignment of AI in mathematics: 25 Fields medalists sign the declaration

    Twenty-five Fields medalists (Tao, Scholze, Avila, Bhargava, Viazovska among them) signed a public declaration at mathandai.org arguing that the AI industry's benchmark race to solve math problems is misaligned with the math community's goal of understanding. Solving is a proxy, they say — the mass production of true/false statements at ever-faster pace could destroy the slow community process of talks, writeups, and simplification that turns a proof into shared insight.

    • The argument
      • Famous problems are landmarks for measuring new understanding; the writeup-and-simplification process, not the solved checkbox, is the actual product.
      • Rushed announcements skip citation and method isolation, raising attribution and plagiarism questions.
      • Without human mathematicians willing to develop AI-conceived ideas, 'the crucial human transmission chain between mathematicians would be lost.'
    • The flashpoint
      • The thread's fight is over the word 'misalignment': critics say it implies deliberate sabotage and misuses AI-safety jargon for what is really a capability gap.
      • The rebuttal: the misalignment is between AI companies' goals (publicity ahead of IPOs) and the community's — a legitimate, literal use of the term.
      • This lands right after the Andreas Thom/OpenAI row over private chat sessions feeding unpublished-math results — which is why 'who will share work with a lab-owned model again?' is the thread's refrain.
    • The optimists' case
      • The top comment cites Mochizuki's abc saga: a dumped, near-incomprehensible proof still produced conferences, papers, and hallway talks — the community process digests even bad inputs.
      • Terence Tao's sign-on stings in both directions: he's the most prominent AI-in-math power user, so this isn't a Luddite letter.
      • The declaration doesn't oppose AI in math — it pushes for use compatible with the community's pre-existing strengths.

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is an argument about a single word. The top comment tries to deflate the whole declaration — a mathematician offers Mochizuki's abc-conjecture dump as proof that even opaque proofs generate community digestion, and accuses the letter of misusing 'misalignment' to imply evil superintelligences deliberately obfuscating proofs. The rebuttal camp says that's a wild misread: the misalignment is institutional (AI companies' IPO-race publicity goals vs. the math community's), not metaphysical, and nobody claims deliberate sabotage. Both sides keep dragging the declaration back to the OpenAI scooping rows — Thom's unpublished expander-matching work, the Navier-Stokes denial — to ask the thread's real question: given the attribution and plagiarism asymmetries, who would submit promising work to a lab-owned model again?

    • The attack

    • The intent question
      • The report is careful: all evidence is from the public packages; OpenAI's chain-of-thought is internal, so motive is unknown.
      • Targets were public data and presumably scraping infrastructure, not theft — an agent swarm doing recon at ecosystem scale, not a heist.

    I spent $220 on Google app ads — 60% of the installs were robots running sideloaded APKs

    A Dayzle puzzle-app dev turned on Google Ads at CA$40/day for Android installs and found the numbers didn't add up: 56 billed installs, but 33 were bots — 20 on day one running an app version the Play Store had stopped serving, each opening once for zero seconds, 28 phone models across 19 states, all 'installed' from Play per the installer field. The farm watches the shortest ad video without clicking, installs from a saved APK, and Google counts view-then-install as a conversion — a feedback loop that routes more budget to the farm.

    • The mechanics
      • Removing the $1.50 target cost-per-install unlocked spend: Google immediately burned double the daily budget for 21 'installs' — the dev's own panel said 1.
      • Bots install the APK from a saved copy, not the store: faster, and Play can't see it. Every device claims Google Play was the installer.
      • The conversion loop: more farm 'installs' → algorithm thinks the targeting is working → more spend routed to the farm.
    • The defense
      • The dev filed Google's invalid-traffic form and moved the campaign goal from 'install' to 'won a puzzle' — a script can open an app, but solving Sudoku costs the farm more.
      • Theory: make your app more expensive to farm than the next one. Larger apps, he guesses, get far more of this than they detect.
    • The takeaway
      • Google's install count is a real number — but don't trust it without your own analytics.
      • If a bot farm found a $220 micro-budget, it can find yours.

    Google is rewriting search result links as opaque google.com/goto URLs — anti-scraping, one redirect at a time

    Google Search now rewrites organic result links as google.com/goto?url=<opaque blob> instead of the old google.com/url?q=<readable target>, and the blob is a Google-specific encoding you can't decode offline. The real destination only appears in the Location header. As of late August 2026 it's consistent for logged-out and private sessions — each result now costs a request back to Google to resolve, which is slower, noisier, and tells Google exactly who's bulk-resolving hundreds of links.

    • The change
      • Old format: target readable in the query string. New: opaque reference to Google's index record; request /goto, read Location, do NOT follow.
      • Still noisier than scraping: every resolved link is a signal to Google's abuse systems.
      • Pairs with removing &num=100 and tightening BotGuard/SearchGuard — a steady ratchet against SERP harvesting.
    • The target
      • AI crawlers and SEO scrapers that bulk-extract result URLs to build their own indexes — with plaintext links, thousands of URLs could be harvested from one HTML fetch.
      • For anyone building a search index from SERP data at scale, the unit economics just got worse.
    • The cat and mouse
      • Autom already updated its Google Search pipeline to resolve goto links server-side, so API customers keep getting destination URLs unchanged.
      • The author expects the redirect format to keep shifting; watch the rollout before assuming it's stable.
    Source: www.autom.dev · Discuss: HN Discuss · 501 pts / 400 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is the Google-shittification ritual in full swing: the top comment mourns that 'Google died like 15 years ago' when it shifted from returning websites to returning answers, and several commenters defect to Yandex or Kagi for 'old Google, but modern.'     A Design Space Exploration of Async/Await: nine dimensions where async runtimes actually differ

    A Brown CS blog post maps the design space of async/await across nine dimensions — the fundamental implementation choices that separate async runtimes. Async looks self-contained and simple until you enumerate the decisions (function color, scheduling, cancellation, effects, etc.) and realize the trade-offs are still not fully understood collectively. A rare semantics-first comparative piece, and the thread treats it as a small event.

    • The nine dimensions
      • The point isn't the nine — it's that each dimension's implications are subtle, under-studied, and have wide consequences for the whole language.
      • Async is deceptive: it presents as a small language feature and turns out to be a systems-design problem.
    • The colored-functions fight
      • The classic JS pain: a sync function can call async but can't use the result — the infection spreads up the call tree and refactors are breaking.
      • One camp: design around it — separate async and sync call trees, make callers supply data up front; hiding I/O behind sync signatures just produces N+1 queries you can't see.
      • The Effect camp: explicit effect runtimes are the principled fix; the LLM camp: function-color mismatch is now a cheap refactor away.
    • Why people care
      • Commenters who had semantics-first PL courses (concurrency, modules, evaluation order, exceptions, lineage) say it taught them more than any single-language course.
      • The thread is basically an alumni reunion for that way of thinking.
    Source: cel.cs.brown.edu · Discuss: HN Discuss · 349 pts / 93 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's energy goes straight to the colored-functions problem: the inability to await from a sync context is the lived pain of async/await, and the discussion splits between the architectural-discipline camp (keep async and sync call trees separate, push data-fetching to callers, never hide network calls behind sync signatures — 'you can't even tell that's happening from the function signature') and the architecture-adaptability camp (a hallmark of good architecture is surviving unexpected changes; demanding developers be omniscient about which functions will need I/O is not a plan). Effect gets the principled-system nod, LLMs get the pragmatist nod ('function color mismatch is no longer a problem while explicit IO helps to read'). The nine dimensions themselves get reverence but less debate — the semantics-first comparison is the point.

    Five years operating petabyte-scale ClickHouse clusters: bring a DBA, gate the queries, fear the noisy neighbor

    A Tinybird engineer distills five years of operating petabyte-scale ClickHouse: at 20k+ rows/s with humans pushing changes, you need a full-time person managing the cluster and — more importantly — managing the queries. The headline lesson is organizational, not technical: someone has to act as gatekeeper and rate-limiter on schemas and queries, because a single bad query pattern can take the whole cluster down with timeouts and OOMs.

    • The operational reality
      • Over 20k rows/s with people pushing changes = a full-time cluster handler, mostly for 'the crazy queries people are going to write.'
      • Scaling compute is easy; partitioning load so one bad access pattern doesn't knock everyone over is the hard part.
      • DBA culture's real value wasn't writing better queries — it was functioning as a rate-limiter on what could exist.
    • The counterargument
      • DuckDB against S3 scales shockingly far for 99.9% of use cases — separation of compute and storage commoditizes the easy wins.
      • Iceberg/Delta Lake partition the data and randomize key prefixes; you can pay Amazon linearly for more reads.
      • The rebuttal: '99.9% of people reading this will never operate a system at that scale' — and noisy-neighbor protection is the part that doesn't commoditize.
    • The human cost
      • The bitterest thread: we're taking a normal developer and making them the database expert, cloud expert, on-call, Linux-log debugger, AWS-cost optimizer — 'and we pay them the exact same.'
      • Two truths coexist: devs should meet a minimum bar of environment knowledge, and management treating it as a dichotomy (experts-as-bottleneck vs. devs-do-everything) breaks both ways.
    Source: www.tinybird.co · Discuss: HN Discuss · 232 pts / 82 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is really about the death of the DBA and what replaced it. The top comment argues DBA culture's benefit was being a gatekeeper and rate-limiter — managed DBs obsoleted the maintenance work, but nothing replaced the 'you may not run that query' function. The DuckDB camp says compute/storage separation made the whole debate moot for 99.9% of workloads ('you'd be shocked how well duckdb against s3 scales'), and the Iceberg camp adds that data lakes partition what used to be the hard part. But the scale-out rebuttal sticks: scaling reads is linear; surviving one team's bad query without taking down the other nine teams is not. Underneath, the angriest comments are about labor: the industry quietly added DBA, cloud, on-call, and cost-optimization to the developer job description at the same salary.
