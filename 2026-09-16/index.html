    An e-ink frame that hears birds and draws them as 1800s illustrations

    A Show HN that landed at #1 by a mile: a microphone in the garden, BirdNET-Go (open-source BirdNET classifier) detecting birds by sound, and an e-ink frame that draws a real 1800s natural-history illustration of each one it hears. 800+ cutouts across 400+ species cut from public-domain plates, dithered to six colors. Runs fully local on a Raspberry Pi. The catch in the thread: it looks like a fork of Teddy Warner's viral AvianVisitors, and the author didn't credit it.

    • What it does
      • Screen redraws only when the detected bird set changes; collage dithered down to six colors for the e-ink panel. Bigger birds sit toward the center, scaled by real body mass.
      • All art is historic and human-made; coverage best for Nordics, Britain, and Germany. Live web demo running from the author's garden in Bergen, Norway.
      • E-ink panel is optional — it can run web-only in a container against a BirdNET-Go you already have.
    • The friction
      • Attribution complaint: commenters flag the project as a fork of Twarner491/AvianVisitors, which went viral a few months ago — "not mentioning it as an inspiration is a very ugly move."
      • E-ink economics: the 13-inch panel is £229.50 — the display costs more than the rest of the build.
    • The read
      • "One of the most wonderful uses of AI I've seen in a while" — classification-only ML, running locally, doing one charming thing well.
      • Multiple commenters plan to build one for parents — the rare HN project whose audience is your mom.

    System One Models and Jev — a frontier model that can't generate text

    TypeSafe AI (founder Diogo Almeida, ex-OpenAI, helped build the instruction-following methods behind ChatGPT) came out of two years in stealth with a new class of model: no string generation, just typed probabilistic decisions. Jev, the first public model in early access, claims similar intelligence to LLMs on structured tasks at two orders of magnitude less cost — $0.042/MTok in, output tokens free — with 70–500ms responses and honestly calibrated probabilities. Kahneman's System 1 is the namesake; Jevons the product name.

    • The pitch
      • Unstructured program state in, type-safe structured decisions out — never hallucinates, never a type error. Training method called RLCD (Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions).
      • Workflow evals claim 193.6x faster and 444.6x cheaper than frontier LLMs on System One shaped tasks, with the reference being the average of GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1.
      • Demos: Doom playing at 10 queries/sec (cost ~$7/hour) and wikiracing — both structured-state tasks, not vision yet.
    • The receipts
      • HN rewards the FAQ's honesty: own evals "run from our laptops on the West Coast," speedups on wikiracing use non-reasoning modes of competitors, pricing "can't prove it isn't subsidized."
      • Jev supports cardinality up to 255; higher-cardinality choices get a two-stage score-then-choose, with the occasional slowdown disclosed.
    • The thread
      • "Wild that it doesn't generate text" — comparisons to Tesla FSD's non-language stack; one commenter wants to feed it an AST for coding.
      • Beta signup optimism: could replace 40–70% of LLM calls in a pipeline and cut those API costs by an order of magnitude.
    Source: typesafe.ai · Discuss: HN Discuss · 1581 pts / 438 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's reception is unusually warm for a model launch, and the top-voted sentiment is curiosity rather than dunking. "This sort of stuff almost sends shivers down my spine, it's like i'm looking 5 years into the future." The serious engagement is about fit: no text generation means no chat, no code writing directly — one commenter suggests feeding it an AST for coding, another compares it to Tesla's FSD stack. The pragmatic thread runs the numbers: if it replaces 40–70% of LLM pipeline calls at two orders of magnitude less cost, the economics alone justify beta testing. The founder's FAQ gets the kind of praise HN reserves for self-skeptical founders — "Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence so see below for the receipts" is the single quoted line. Nobody verified the evals; everyone noted the honesty of the nuance sections.

    Wayback Machine buckles under scraper waves — bots vs. the archive

    Internet Archive published an update: the Wayback Machine is getting hit by waves of high-volume automated traffic, and their new protections are throttling legit users too — moving a mouse on the calendar from a residential IP can now earn a 429. The top comment's theory is brutal: scrapers are working around blocks on original sites by hitting the Wayback copy instead, and some sites are now opting out of the archive entirely to prevent it.

    • What's happening
      • Waves of high-volume automated traffic; Archive put protections in place to keep the service running. If you think you were blocked in error, email info@archive.org with your OS, browser, and IP.
      • False positives are real: residential IPs get stuck on 429s from light use; corporate ISPs and airport WiFi often can't reach web.archive.org at all.
    • The scraper theory
      • "I'm pretty certain this is scrapers that are trying to workaround blocks on accessing original sites by hitting the Wayback Machine copy instead. Appalling behavior."
      • Second-order damage: some sites have opted out of the Wayback Machine to prevent their content from being scraped via the alternative route — the archive shrinks because scrapers treat it as a CDN.
    • The proposals
      • Paid endpoint for crawlers; downloader-pays S3 model; one commenter floats Anubis (proof-of-work challenge) as the inevitable endgame.
      • "AI companies should pay billions to wayback machine for access" — the vibes-based funding plan.
    Source: blog.archive.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 603 pts / 326 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread runs on two tracks. Track one is the diagnosis: simonw's comment dominates — scrapers routed around site-level blocks are laundering their crawls through the archive, and the collateral damage is twofold (archive overload plus sites opting out of the Wayback Machine, degrading the archive itself). Track two is the lived experience of the protections: 429s from ordinary residential use, corporate IPs blocked wholesale, the "it works on my phone but not my work PC" confusion. The proposals are the usual mix of economics and engineering — charge the scrapers, downloader-pays storage, Anubis — but nobody believes AI labs are about to write the Archive a check. The emotional register is protective: this is a vital non-profit piece of internet infrastructure, and the anger is aimed squarely at whoever is treating it as free compute.

    Norway's consumer council: "Let's make quality the norm again"

    Forbrukerrådet (the Norwegian Consumer Council) launched a short-life campaign for product longevity, and HN took it as a prompt to relitigate why everything feels flimsier. The leading theory: quality decline is hidden inflation — the thing still costs X while regulations added cost Y, so production moved to China and inputs got scrimped. The hard part: even shoppers willing to pay 10x for quality can't find anything worth buying.

    • The symptom
      • Clothing is the consensus example: "after the first wash already looks worn out, terrible or starts falling apart" — from major known brands, regardless of price.
      • Closets full of junk you look terrible in anyway; people want shops focused on quality fabrics and sewing processes, but they don't exist.
    • The theories
      • Hidden inflation: same price X, higher regulatory cost Y — quality is the slack variable.
      • Brand cash-out: quality brands are incentivized to sell out the brand name since customers take time to catch on; ephemeral no-name brands make quality unmarketable.
      • Subcontractor concentration: as subcontractors grow huge, they control the quality ceiling of the entire market; complaints never reach the factory.
    • The pushback
      • Software angle: quality would mean certified developers, third-party validation of every patch, warranties on commercial software — raising prices and eliminating most of the industry.
      • One contrarian: the fragile state of global affairs worries them more than T-shirt quality; AGI is the exit.
    Source: forbrukerradet.no · Discuss: HN Discuss · 437 pts / 443 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is a diagnostic exercise more than a policy debate. The most-upvoted commenter reframes the whole thing as macroeconomics: it's not that products are designed to be cheap, it's that price is sticky and cost isn't, so quality is the release valve. The artisans-are-dead anecdote (a Polish carpenter who'd rather cut up pre-made cabinets than build from wood, even at 10x) grounds it. The cynical model — quality brands cash out on reputation, no-name brands make premium pricing look like a scam — gets strong agreement. And the software comparison lands the thread's coldest take: in an unregulated industry, real quality would mean certification, validated patches, and warranties — i.e., software would cost more and most developers would be unemployable. The Norwegian-source mockery ("Thank god for the little norwegians living in their utopian bubble") is present but outvoted by people who'd pay up if they could.

    A working Linux GPU driver for the M4 Mac Mini — built in a month, mostly by Codex

    Cody Ho and Niklas built a fully OpenGL ES 3.0 compliant GPU driver for the M4 Mac Mini (and MacBook Neo) in about a month — a task that normally takes years — by pointing Codex at Apple's AGX firmware ABI via a hypervisor they built for capture-and-replay reverse engineering. Minecraft runs at 200fps. The thread's fight isn't about the engineering — it's about whether anyone can touch the code: the author is ex-Apple, and Asahi Linux has a strictly no-AI policy.

    • The engineering
      • Reverse engineered the M4/A18 Pro (mostly M5) user space plus the full AGX firmware ABI — RTKit firmware, shared memory structs — using only live hypervisor traces, "not looked at any Apple binaries."
      • Codex's signature move: capture a GPU memory snapshot, reboot, replay the whole memory state back in, then shrink the replayed pages until everything is built from source. Compute was the hard part (336MB unreplayable capture; fixed via single-user-mode tiny traces).
      • Kernel driver ported to async Rust in three days; custom shader compiler, command stream builder, Mesa frontend.
    • The controversy
      • Ex-Apple author taint: "All this work is tainted since the poster is ex-Apple" — Apple people also contribute to Linux; ongoing Apple-vs-OpenAI trade-secrets lawsuit cited.
      • Asahi Linux's no-AI policy means this can't be upstreamed there — "a bunch of AI-assisted forks that get things working smoothly" are expected to dominate anyway.
      • The clean-room trap: showing a page of LLM-derived firmware ABI without disclosing the LLM use until late is "incredibly rude" to anyone trying to stay clean-room.
    • The consensus
      • "You don't need someone to spend years reverse engineering undocumented hardware anymore" — the thread treats this as the best LLM use case yet.
      • Codex's taste got the praise: "good taste regarding when it should poke the hardware some more and when it should just run the hypervisor."
    Source: codyho.dev · Discuss: HN Discuss · 380 pts / 224 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is a three-way argument. The optimists call it a watershed: LLM-driven hardware RE collapses a multi-year grind into weeks, and the hypervisor-plus-replay methodology is the real deliverable. The legal realists kill the celebration: an ex-Apple author plus Apple suing OpenAI over stolen trade secrets plus Asahi's no-AI policy means this code can likely never be upstreamed, however impressive — "There is no way Linux will take that code." The most surgical criticism is from kmeisthax: publishing LLM-derived firmware ABI before disclosing the LLM use is a trap for anyone working clean-room. Underneath, one line of genuine philosophy: "Figuring out how the hardware works and how to interface with it to submit work is a significant step all by itself."

    Apple Reference Image — verified photos, and the replay attack that kills it

    Apple's security blog announced Reference Image: cryptographically verified photography for iPhone. HN needed about five minutes to find the hole: generate an AI image, display it on a high-res monitor, photograph the monitor with an iPhone 18 Pro — valid Apple Reference image. Cardboard box, Vantablack interior, LiDAR tape, and you can have "Apple Verified" UFOs over the Golden Gate Bridge.

    • The idea
      • Verified photography — an Apple-blessed "certified real" tag on photos, presumably for the deepfake era.
      • One concern: using it at all means sending your image to Apple's PCC (Private Cloud Compute) machines.
    • The attacks
      • The replay: photograph an already-edited or AI-generated image on another display. "What if someone take the photo of the forged photo displayed on another device, doesn't the forged photo become an authentic one?"
      • The hardened version: cardboard box to block external light, Vantablack interior against reflections, LiDAR projector taped. The tag survives; only the scene's authenticity was the question.
    • The real problem
      • Not technical: "people will see the 'certified real' tag and just take the image for face value... their brain will short circuit." A verified badge amplifies the misleading frame it certifies.
      • "A photograph by itself should never be considered proof of anything" — and now the proof comes with Apple's brand on it. "NFTs by another name."
    Source: security.apple.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 353 pts / 237 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's energy is adversarial-engineering-as-sport: the replay attack was found almost instantly, then refined (Vantablack, LiDAR tape, the cardboard box) into a joke spec for manufacturing verified UFO photos. But the highest-value comment reframes the whole product: the failure mode isn't that the crypto can be broken — it's that a "Real Photo, Verified by Apple" badge will be read by normal humans as "true," short-circuiting the skepticism the badge was supposed to institutionalize. The privacy thread (images routed to Apple's PCC) and the NFT comparison both got traction, but the dominant verdict is that Apple built a trust primitive that degrades the thing it certifies: scenes are verifiable, context never is.

    Still bearish on LLMs after Navier-Stokes — the rigorous-spec bottleneck

    Jay Kruer's post: frontier labs are priced as if they sell drop-in replacements for knowledge workers, but models need "laborious oversight and guardrails on even the simplest tasks." The core argument is structural, not vibe-based: reward hacking can only be fixed by rigorous specification by domain experts — expensive, rare, and frequently more costly than implementation. Navier-Stokes in Lean is the rosiest possible setup, and the vast majority of knowledge work doesn't look like it.

    • The theses
      • Models generalize only within a small neighborhood of trained tasks; small perturbations produce outright failure or reward hacking.
      • Rigorous spec needs domain experts; the expert-specifier intersection is "ludicrously small," and spec costs can exceed implementation — CPU projects run ~3-5x more spec/validation engineers than designers.
      • Human review is the alternative and it doesn't scale — see the xz backdoor and UMN hypocrite commits landing in Linux.
    • The three-firm taxonomy
      • Only three classes of firms can accept fully autonomous LLMs: those that can accept cheap failure (interns, prototyping), narrow well-guardrailed tasks (call centers), and those that already pay for rigorous spec (chip design, drug discovery).
      • For the first two, cheap open models suffice — and swarm width matters more than reasoning for the fuzzy combinatorial search behind the headline results.
    • The punchline
      • LLMs will look like "a cracked intern: quick and effective in the hands of an adult but not given run of the place." The data-center-of-brainlets scenario is bottlenecked by human orchestrators, so the blast radius goes beyond frontier labs.
    Source: dank.systems · Discuss: HN Discuss · 303 pts / 393 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's dominant reaction is relief: a tempered bearishness that's "not literally denialist about current capabilities." The most-cited evidence is external — an April 2026 paper where frontier models, asked to play chess, couldn't identify legal moves better than 80% of the time and none beat an 1100-ELO bot. The consensus refinement: bearish on LLMs for automation, bullish on LLM-plus-human experts in narrow domains — humans as the "temporal tethers" for models that can't estimate real-world time. One economic thread lands cleanly: "Open and cheap models will undercut the big labs continuously. The blast radius won't be pretty once spending commitments knock the door." The doomers and the hype men are both present but neither owns the thread; the pragmatists do.

    We got admin access to Baseten's production GitHub — via a 2023 Docker image

    Strix disclosed how they landed admin access to Baseten's production GitHub: a live GitHub PAT embedded in the build metadata of a public Harbor Docker image dated March 2023 — still working in July 2026. It had admin and push access to the main product repo, the GitOps repo driving their clusters, the Homebrew tap, plus read/write to private customer-specific repos. Baseten rotated it within hours and sent the researchers merch. HN's verdict: "great marketing for strix, pretty bad for baseten."

    • The find
      • Token leaked via Docker build args embedded in image metadata — use BuildKit secrets instead, or --provenance=false to strip build metadata.
      • Scope: admin+push on main product repo, cluster-driving GitOps repo, Homebrew tap; read/write on other private repos including customer-specific ones.
      • The image dated to March 2023; the token still worked in July 2026 — a three-year live credential.
    • The response
      • Timeline: reported Jul 13 11:10 PM; Harbor project made private next morning; token rotated Jul 14 4:34 PM; remaining findings closed Jul 17; disclosed publicly in September with a draft shared first.
      • Baseten sent t-shirts and sweatshirts as thanks. One prospective customer: "kudos to their team for the fast patch" — but was evaluating them as an inference provider.
    • The awkward part
      • Baseten's front page carries SOC2 Type II and HIPAA compliance logos, with customers like OpenEvidence (2/3 of US physicians) and Harvey (legal). "What are the legal implications here?"
      • Symptom, not one-off: "points to certain experience and expertise level for security practices" — startups ship fast, and this is what the shortcut looks like in production.
    Source: strix.ai · Discuss: HN Discuss · 303 pts / 172 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread reads as a case study with two ledgers. Ledger one is process: responsible disclosure done well — reported same night, rotated within a day, draft shared before publication, even the merch gets noted. Ledger two is existential: a live three-year-old token with admin on the main repo and the GitOps repo that drives production clusters, at a company advertising SOC2 Type II and HIPAA to healthcare and legal customers. The most-upvoted commentary notes the inversion — a startup validating its provider's security and finding this — and asks what default security boundaries vibecoded internal agents should follow so the next Harbor-like project doesn't ship a credential. The security folks add the practical fix (build secrets, --provenance=false); the cynics add the honest one: it's great marketing for strix, pretty bad for baseten.
