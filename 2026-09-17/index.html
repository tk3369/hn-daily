    Nvidia announces native GPU programming in Rust

    Nvidia ships two Rust tracks for writing GPU kernels natively: SIMT via cuda-oxide (custom rustc codegen backend, nightly) and Tile via cutile-rs (stable Rust, JIT through Tile IR). The pitch is compile-time safety — data races and aliasing become borrow-checker errors. Still Nvidia-only hardware, though.

    • What shipped
      • SIMT: cuda-oxide, rustc backend to PTX
      • Tile: cutile-rs, tile-level JIT, stable Rust
    • HN take
      • Safety story praised; CUDA lock-in dreaded
      • 'Trojan horse': hard to remove once in your code
    Source: developer.nvidia.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 802 pts / 326 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is guarded: the compile-time safety story (kernel races caught as borrow-checker errors) is admired, but a large faction says it doesn't matter because CUDA itself is the problem — 'a cleverly designed Trojan horse' that's nearly impossible to remove once it infects a codebase, ending in #ifdef hell or single-vendor lock. Commenters argue the better approach is kernels in separate files with a separate compile step (Metal/OpenCL/Triton style), noting Nvidia's ISA changes frequently so direct ISA targeting stays unrealistic. Part of the discussion derailed into a debate about self-censored typing.

    Training a 4B model to produce 81% faster query plans than Postgres

    Rohan Bansal trained a 4B model (Qwen) to generate query plans 81% faster than Postgres' planner on his benchmark. It shows learned cost models can beat decades of heuristics — but the 8GB all-in-memory, warmed, read-only workload invites skepticism about real OLTP with drift and cold data.

    • What it is
      • 4B model learns query optimization; 81% better than Postgres planner
    • HN take
      • Skeptics: warmed 8GB dataset, drift kills plans
      • Postgres' new query hints could make this an extension
    Source: rohanbansal.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 610 pts / 125 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingConsensus: impressive experiment, deserved kudos for getting hands dirty. Skepticism dominates the analysis: the headline's '81% faster' rests on an 8GB dataset that fits entirely in memory, constrained shared_buffers, warmed runs, and read-only SELECTs — classic profile-guided optimization conditions where workload drift at scale can erase the gains. Debate splits on where GPUs belong: one camp asks why not CUDA-accelerate Postgres itself (joins, sorts, and hashing parallelize well), the other notes PCIe and disk realities and that Postgres already plans against actual parameter values. Several note Postgres' new query hints could make this an extension.

    Backups Aren't Simple

    Filip Filipovski's essay on why backups fail, grounded in real data-loss stories — including losing data *during* a backup. The thread piles on: OneDrive folder hijacking, SD controller deaths, lightning. Throughline: backups fail in the details — unplugged copies and append-only tooling beat the medium.

    • What it is
      • Essay on backup failure modes from four war stories
    • HN take
      • One backup unplugged; restic append-only praised
      • Object lock beats Blu-ray; used LTO tape still competitive
    Source: filipovski.net · Discuss: HN Discuss · 281 pts / 176 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingConsensus: backups are a process, not a product. The top comment's four data-loss stories — lightning through a fax modem, a OneDrive terms change plus throttled downloads, a dead SD controller on holiday, and data lost while performing the backup itself — set the tone, and the thread's lesson is that rewritable media needs one copy fully unplugged while another updates. The tool debate is civil and practical: restic with append-only rules and systemd restrictions gets praise, object lock on object stores wins over Blu-ray for ransomware resistance (one counterpoint: the provider could shut down), and tape fans note used LTO-5 drives around $300 with ~$20 2TB cartridges. OneDrive catches heavy fire for hijacking Windows folders and quietly deleting files after limit cuts.

    The engineering behind the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve

    John Wang's deep dive on the SPR: oil stored in underground salt caverns, where salt self-seals fractures and water pumped into the bottom pushes oil out the top. Elegant, chemistry-driven infrastructure most people never see — plus a GAO report held up as a gold standard for technical writing.

    • What it is
      • SPR stores oil in salt caverns, displaced by water
    • HN take
      • Salt self-seals; presaturated brine stops leaching
      • GAO report: model for documenting technical decisions
    Source: johnjwang.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 235 pts / 97 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingGenuinely educational thread. The top discussion unpacks the physics: salt's low permeability and self-deforming behavior seal fractures without steel linings, oil floats on water so pumping water in displaces oil upward, and an informed commenter corrects that leaching is controlled by pumping presaturated brine, with brine ponds visible on satellite imagery. A question about open-air storage draws crisp answers — crude oil offgasses VOCs, evaporates, and invites lightning and attack. Multiple commenters praise the cited GAO report as a gold standard for documenting complex technical decisions for non-technical readers, with one defending the quality of civil-service engineering work.

    Keys Not Included: recovering the signing keys for US driver's license barcodes

    Ryan Fahey reverse-engineered the undocumented ECDSA signatures in New York and Virginia license barcodes, recovering each state's public key — and showed a counterfeit fails instantly. California publishes its keys; the blocker was never engineering.

    • What it is
      • Recovered P-256 public keys for NY/VA license barcodes
    • Security implication
      • Counterfeits fail — but cloned genuine barcodes still pass
      • IDEMIA ships verifiable sigs in only 1 of 31 states
    Source: ryan.science · Discuss: HN Discuss · 224 pts / 87 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingConsensus: impressive reverse engineering, and the conclusion lands — recovered keys let anyone verify NY/VA barcodes, a well-formed counterfeit fails the check instantly, and publishing public keys is their entire purpose. The strongest agreement is on the article's moral: California (via IDEMIA) proved the open system works at the largest-state scale, yet IDEMIA ships it in only 1 of 31 jurisdictions, so the blocker is willingness, not engineering. One noted limitation: copying a genuine barcode onto a counterfeit card still passes, since the photo isn't signed. The liveliest debate is pedagogical — what to call public keys ('lock', 'address', 'public-text') after years of the flawed key analogy.
