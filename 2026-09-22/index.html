    I don't want to read what you didn't write: the case against AI-authored docs

    Colin Breck's essay argues AI-generated design docs, PR descriptions, tickets, and meeting summaries are unreadable — machine-retrospective summaries of already-built things, rich in detail but void of context or perspective. The core asymmetry: the prompter has context and can skim; the reader has none and must read exhaustively. AI's real value in writing is verification — checking prose against source code, logs, and metrics — not generation.

    • The argument
      • 'Readers shouldn't labor to understand a sentence the writer didn't work to create' (Cantrill, quoted)
      • Survey data: 78% stop reading AI-scented posts, 71% avoid the author
    • Where AI helps
      • Ruthless grammar checking, citations, TikZ diagrams; it caught a notation bug four human reviewers missed
      • Best at the abstract — 'the most terse, mechanical, inhuman part'
    • HN take
      • Broad agreement: writing is brain-to-brain transfer; 300 LLM bits can't fill 700 missing ones
      • Contrarians: an LLM sparring partner is fine if you proofread and it sounds like you
      • Noted irony: the essay's own first paragraph reads AI-generated
    Source: blog.colinbreck.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 827 pts / 345 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingStrong agreement with the core claim: 'Writing is fundamentally the transfer of information from your brain to my brain' — if you give an LLM 300 bits of semantic information, it can't invent the other 700, because it doesn't know what they are. Practitioners share the pain: reviewers pushing back on PRs because a 20-line change now ships with pages of generated rationalization, 'and you put me in the position where I can't afford not to' read it. The contrarian camp defends LLM-assisted writing done right — proofread, re-prompted until it sounds like a human, genuinely conveying the information — and argues AI gives a voice to people who 'do their thinking' in non-prose forms. The spiciest thread is self-referential: the essay's own first sentence is flagged as exactly what it laments, and one commenter notes the author later AI-summarized a reader's critique as the response. Pangram detection and Oxide's human-authored mandate get discussed as emerging social infrastructure.

    Spymarks, not watermarks: naming the hidden tracking signals in your media

    Brandon Thomas coins 'spymark' for hidden tracking signals embedded in media — Google SynthID's imperceptible marks carrying 136-bit payloads (a 64-bit database ID) in images, audio, and text — as distinct from benign visible watermarks. Unlike EXIF, spymarks are invisible, survive compression and metadata stripping, and serve the embedder, not you. Interactive demos walk through image, audio, and text spymarks.

    • The definition
      • Spy- (involuntary surveillance) + -mark (embedded signal): a privacy Rumpelstiltskin
      • Printer tracking dots from the 1980s are the original spymark
    • The risk
      • Corporations once spymarked internal pages to identify leakers from screenshots
      • A world of account-linked spymarks on every post would make hunting whistleblowers trivial
    • HN take
      • 'It's just steganography' vs the naming gambit has real rhetorical power
      • Ad-attribution dystopia: intercepting images on the way to the display
      • Criticism: live demos are vibecoded toy examples, not real SynthID
    Source: brand.io · Discuss: HN Discuss · 554 pts / 133 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread splits between taxonomy and dread. The 'it's just steganography' camp sees nothing new; the other camp grants that naming matters — 'to speak the name is to control the thing' — and that spymark puts the privacy concern up front where 'watermark' never could. The darkest subthread is advertising: spymarks are 'going to be very popular for intercepting images on their way to a display,' with ad attribution 'vastly improved' when every funnel step is spymarked and reported by low-level drivers — a world several commenters find indistinguishable from 1984. Defenses discussed: byte-for-byte identical trusted pipelines (trusted camera, editor, compressor), and journalistic traditions of transcribing/redrawing screenshots to protect leakers — 'a whole fun adversarial loop.' The article's demos take friendly fire: 'not SynthID' toy examples, a generic spectrogram, an ID space of 173 — one reader 'hoped to see more realistic scenarios.'

    Can gzip be a language model? Compression as a plausibility tester

    Nathan's essay treats gzip as a language model: compression is prediction, so feeding a prompt to gzip and searching for the continuation that compresses best gives a lower bound on 'language modeling.' A clean demonstration of the Kolmogorov/NCD idea — classifying text by which corpus it compresses best against — with honest limits: the searchable space is astronomically smaller than the space of possible sequences.

    • The idea
      • Compression = prediction; the best-compressing continuation is the most plausible
      • Classic trick: gzip sports.txt + testfile to classify topic by resulting size
    • The limit
      • The result is only a lower bound — we can't search a meaningful slice of the sequence space
    • HN take
      • Witten's Waikato work cited as the original version
      • 3blue1brown's series recommended as the companion explainer
      • 'Winrar more profitable than OpenAI — coincidence? I think not.'
    Source: nathan.rs · Discuss: HN Discuss · 264 pts / 93 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingShort and fond. The classic demo gets restated in full: 'gzip -9 sports.txt testfile.txt' against domain corpora of equal size, test file goes to the topic with the smallest .gz — credited to Witten's group at Waikato. The honest-limits crowd converges on the search-space problem: the continuation that compresses best is only a lower bound on what gzip 'knows,' because there's no way to search a meaningful part of the sequence space. 3blue1brown's LLM series is the recommended companion viewing. One historical caveat gets upvoted: 'people have gone a bit overboard saying models like this, or n-gram models, are anywhere close to large neural network models' — there's a connection, but it's not equivalence. The thread's peak comment is the joke: 'This tracks perfectly with Winrar being more profitable than OpenAI... coincidence? I think not!'
