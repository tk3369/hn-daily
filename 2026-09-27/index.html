    PipePipe: NewPipe hard fork implementing SponsorBlock — now fully independent

    PipePipe forked NewPipe in early 2022 over differences in development philosophy and went fully independent — no upstream merges in either direction. The pitch: NewPipe reimagined, faster and more stable, with SponsorBlock auto-skip (YouTube + BiliBili), restored dislikes via ReturnYouTubeDislike, AV1/VP9, danmaku overlays, and background music mode. 6.4k stars, GPL-3.0, on F-Droid.

    • What it adds
      • SponsorBlock auto-skip of sponsored segments (YouTube & BiliBili); ReturnYouTubeDislike restores dislike counts
      • AV1/VP9 codecs, danmaku-style live chat overlays, music-player mode with background playback
      • Keyword/channel filters, blocks Shorts, gesture controls, sleep timer, bulk playlist downloads
    • The hard-fork bet
      • Independent development = fast fixes without upstream coordination — but no free security/capability merges from NewPipe either
      • Forks like Tubular chose the opposite: track NewPipe upstream and build on top
    • HN take
      • Thread immediately became a P2P-caching wishlist: ten people watching the same video should mean one download from YouTube
      • Consensus that P2P video died on mobile batteries, CGNAT, and asymmetric upload — plus BitTorrent would publish your watch history
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 455 pts / 240 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread barely discusses the app and instantly becomes a referendum on P2P video. The wishlist: federated/P2P caching so ten people watching the same video means one download from YouTube's servers, then add direct uploads and go fully independent. The rebuttal is well-worn and convincing: P2P died because upload is terrible outside fiber markets, HTTPS CDNs got dirt cheap, everyone watches on phones behind CGNAT, and BitTorrent would publish your watch history to the internet. A side skirmish on whether Europe's upload is actually worse than the US ends with: irrelevant, the phone kills it either way.

    Fifteen years later, the Apple Cards origin story: invisible barcodes, a shoebox of demand

    Lex Friedman talked to 'Mike,' who ran Apple's print-fulfillment program: Cards (Oct 2011) was a Steve Jobs idea — 'wouldn't it be great if I could right now on my iPhone send a thank-you card?' — codenamed Speed Racer. Apple demanded letterpress on toothy cotton stock, invisible UV barcodes so envelopes stayed 'unadulterated,' got USPS to scan every card at every step, and issued a custom heart stamp. Launch day: 'the global demand for Cards could fit in a shoebox.'

    • The Jobs tax on logistics
      • Three-pass printing (pre-coat, letterpress shell, digital imprint) because ink wouldn't adhere to cotton stock; restored 1850s Heidelberg presses
      • Engineers flown in from China; teams sleeping in conference rooms; 'intercontinental mayhem'
    • The restraint failure
      • Apple's forecasting 'was always terrible'; USPS had trucks on site for a glovebox of orders; the post office 'had high hopes' for direct-mail volume that never came
      • Announced Oct 4, 2011 — Jobs died Oct 5; 'no one had the heart to pull the plug' until Sep 2013
    Source: lexontech.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 414 pts / 105 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingHalf war stories, half Apple-process worship. The co-founder of Sincerely (Postagram) shows up: they'd built iPhone-to-printed-card first and felt 'Sherlocked' — but says the announcement only boosted awareness, since Apple Cards was a limited, flawed product with no tracking. The engineering details get loving attention: invisible UV barcodes are mostly standard USPS automation practice, but getting USPS to scan every card at send, facility, and truck was pure force of will. Tangents include Apple sending a camera to the ISS for screensavers and the multi-jurisdiction fight to hide regulatory markings behind an info button. The through-line: Apple spent enormous energy on tactile details users never consciously noticed.

    Does Georgism work? Five years later: the boring scut work is winning

    Lars Doucet — 2021 ACX book-review winner on Progress and Poverty, now full-time at the Center for Land Economics — checks in five years later. The headline: land value tax has its most legislative momentum in decades. Virginia and Kentucky passed enablement laws this April; the UK's new PM and South Korea's president are LVT-friendly; Baden-Württemberg implemented one in Jan 2025. His big lesson: stop trying to win internet arguments — the boring scut work of implementation precedes consensus, not the reverse.

    • The playbook
      • Find officials already sympathetic; 'succeed anywhere' instead of fighting your own city; build case studies as social proof
      • Model who wins/loses with open tools: biggest losers are vacant land and surface parking; the median single-family homeowner wins
    • The AI kicker
      • AI millionaires bidding up SF and Seoul land are making the Georgist case for them — 'land will be a winner in the age of AI'
    • Honest caveats
      • Germany's effective LVT rate is low; the single-tax remains controversial; assessment quality varies by city
    Source: astralcodexten.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 410 pts / 303 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingActivists comparing field notes. The top exchange is a YIMBY organizer's board vote to eliminate single-family zoning: overly online keyboard warriors are the least important people to convince — you never change a staunch NIMBY's mind, you find the already-interested and move them from inactive to active. The Georgist core lands flat with some homeowners: 'if you bought for 50 grand in 1984 and it's worth $3M, that's a jackpot — you did nothing to create that value.' The counter: that equity is retirement money, and dismissing homeowners as a group is ugly. Skeptics note rezoning hasn't cratered values anywhere (Metro DC: pent-up demand absorbs it) — which Georgists count as the design working.

    Show HN: Reladraw – a diagram language where you decide where to place things

    Show HN for a new diagram-as-code language: one generic syntax with deterministic relative placement — you declare where things go relative to each other, no layout engine fighting you. The pitch is the AI-coding age: diagrams as high-bandwidth alignment between your mental model and the agent's. The repo ships an installable skill, and the author fixed real bugs live in the thread.

    • The gap it fills
      • Mermaid is fine for fixed layouts (sequence, Gantt) but bad where position is king (flowcharts); PlantUML is flexible but fiddly
      • Graphviz gives hints, not declarations; Reladraw aims for deterministic relative placement
    • Tradeoffs
      • One generic syntax is both pro and con — mermaid/PlantUML evolved specialized dialects for a reason
      • Absolute positioning still wins for ultra-custom work; relative placement is 'probably enough' for most flowcharts and mind maps
    • Live debugging
      • Commenters found genuine bugs in the playground; the author fixed them in-thread within hours — real goodwill earned
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 344 pts / 90 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingWarm, practical reception from people who've lost hours fiddling with PlantUML relation directions — 'if I had a nickel for every hour...' The most interesting thread is the AI-angle debate: is diagramming even the right human-agent interface, or should we stay at the visual level and have agents communicate visually instead of round-tripping text-to-visual-to-text? Defenders say relative positioning is probably enough without fiddling; skeptics note the repo is basic/rudimentary right now and that deterministic-declarations-vs-hints is a genuinely unexplored design space. The in-thread bug fixes bought the author real credibility.

    Go Concurrency Distilled: the goroutine gospel, annotated

    Anton Zhiyanov's distilled guide to Go concurrency — goroutines, channels, the runtime, no fluff. The thread immediately becomes Go vs. Erlang/BEAM, the eternal HN concurrency debate: WhatsApp's billion users on Erlang vs. Go's 'pick up and play.' Nobody converts anybody; everybody enjoys it.

    • The case for Go
      • Feels like C, self-contained binaries, concurrency as a first-class concept without learning a VM's whole world
      • 'I'm a goroutine addict and I refuse to be rehabilitated'
    • The case for BEAM
      • Fault tolerance by design: nine nines on an Ericsson ATM switch in 2002; WhatsApp, RabbitMQ, Discord
      • 'A masterclass in using concurrency to achieve fault tolerance'
    • The honest trade
      • Go lets you write shared-memory anti-patterns Erlang forbids; Erlang's dynamism and BEAM overhead cost performance and adoption
      • Go wasn't against generics — just against bad ones; Erlang never had them at all
    Source: antonz.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 283 pts / 115 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingA 'goroutine addict' opens, and the BEAM faithful arrive within minutes: WhatsApp's billion users, RabbitMQ, Discord, nine nines on an Ericsson switch in 2002. The best synthesis: Go is for general-purpose programming in big orgs with huge variance in developer experience; Erlang is for fault tolerance as a religion — 'two sides of the same coin' (availability), but Go's trick is pick-up-and-play while BEAM demands you learn its ecosystem to get the concurrency model. The generics sub-thread relitigates old history — Go wasn't against generics, just implementations with bad complexity implications.     DeepSeek Elastic Compute (DSec): 3M sandboxes a day for agentic training

    DeepSeek's new paper describes DSec, the production sandbox platform behind its agentic RL training: FnCall/container/microVM/full-VM backends behind one SDK, on-demand image loading from their 3FS distributed filesystem, RL co-designed scheduling. One production unit: ~160 nodes, 380k concurrent sandboxes, 5k creations/second, ~3M sandboxes/day — infrastructure for agents that routinely try to break containment, including reward hacking.

    • Why it exists
      • Agentic training needs isolated, stateful environments in huge bursts with long-lived state — a single sandbox runtime doesn't cut it
      • Decouples stateful rollouts from preemptible GPU training; preserves rollout state while reclaiming idle resources
    • The numbers
      • 160-node unit: 3M sandboxes/day, 380k concurrent, 5k creations/sec; memory sharing + reclamation for high-density overcommit
    • The meta-discussion
      • 130+ authors (31 not even shown) — asset protection so poachers can't identify the real contributors, or authorship inflation 'akin to academic fraud'?
    Source: arxiv.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 281 pts / 92 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread talks about everything except the technical content — which is itself the story.     How to keep enjoying programming in a world of LLMs

    A Haskell Discourse thread asking the question a lot of programmers are quietly asking. The answers: build things for your kids (LLMs turned months into evenings), treat it like car mechanics — old cars with hand tools, new cars with software patches — or quit the IDE assistance entirely and go back to ctags and grep.

    • Joy-preserving strategies
      • Personal projects for family (drawesome.art, catchmemeow.com) — 'lowering the barrier to proof of concepts is a beautiful thing'
      • Use the LLM as a 'rockstar dev' consultant for suggestions and tradeoffs rather than a code generator
      • Hobby code in a plain text editor; one commenter 'just recently replaced LSP with ctags' and navigates better
    • The anxiety
      • LLM use is measurable in a way 'bouncing a nerf ball off the wall for a week then solving it' never was — and the business side loves to measure
      • Stats that spook people, or get gamed instead of being productive
    • The quiet consensus
      • Using LLMs well is a skill in itself; the joy is in choosing which parts you keep for yourself
    Source: discourse.haskell.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 271 pts / 298 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe most human thread of the day. The car-mechanics analogy lands hardest: previous-generation cars you work on with hand tools, modern cars you tune with software patches — programming is bifurcating the same way. The LLM-skeptics report nothing but bad experiences ('buggy, or it works but I lose an evening on some obscure bug') and have retreated to text editors; the enthusiasts report a wealth of small things built for their kids that would never have existed otherwise. Underneath, a real anxiety: LLM use is measurable, and measurable invites management. The thread's quiet consensus: the skill is using LLMs well, and the joy is in choosing which parts of the craft you keep for yourself.
