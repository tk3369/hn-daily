    Ember-1: Kimi K3 quality with 40% fewer reasoning tokens

Getting there took 50+ training experiments and 200+ evaluations on Fireworks' serverless training platform, validated on external benchmarks, customer A/B tests, and internal coding workloads.

    • What it is
      • Kimi K3 base, trained to cut unnecessary reasoning while keeping the reasoning that matters
      • Targets automated coding workloads where long reasoning traces make inference expensive at scale
      • First in an announced series of specialized models shaped by what developers want next
    • HN take

    • The meta-gripe
      • Multiple comments note every lab now markets a Pareto frontier; 'when everyone's fighting to be somewhere in the pile, they need some way to advertise progress while not being the best'

    Don't couple your Go code to GitHub — own your import namespace

    Go namespaces packages by fetch location, so `import "github.com/foo/bar"` hard-codes your git host into your source. The argument: use your own domain (with a redirect to wherever the code actually lives) so a hosting migration — GitHub to GitLab, a corporate domain reshuffle — is a redirect change, not a code change. One company the author saw got so tangled across GitLab, GitHub, and Azure DevOps that moving hosts was unthinkable.

    • The case
      • Go conflates 'what it is' with 'where it is'; the custom domain separates identity from location
      • Migration then means updating a redirect, not find-replacing import paths across every repo
      • Applies beyond Go: hardcoded host links in comments/docs rot the same way
    • The rebuttal
      • This solves a problem that rarely bites — moving hosts is a find/replace, and you can deprecate the old path with a note
      • Namespaces are human constructs; your custom domain can fail too — 'this namespace can fail! the solution is, use another!'
      • Corporate domain migrations (contoso.com → a VPN-only domain, with /etc/hosts hacks) can be worse than staying on GitHub
    • The vendoring wing
      • For reproducibility, vendor or cache your dependency tree: 'I want to be able to compile critical software when a zombie apocalypse hits'
      • Availability and benevolence of outside parties are both load-bearing assumptions you didn't sign
    Source: iain.rocks · Discuss: HN Discuss · 279 pts / 137 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread splits between 'this is prudent engineering hygiene' and 'this is a hypothetical problem dressed as a real one.' The sharpest counter lands on namespaces being human constructs all the way down — a custom domain is just a different failure mode, and several commenters have lived through corporate domain migrations worse than anything GitHub ever did to them. The vendoring wing redirects the argument to reproducibility: whatever you namespace with, pin your dependency tree locally so a VPN outage doesn't brick your pipeline. Consensus forms around the narrow version: for commercial teams, custom-domain namespacing plus vendoring is cheap insurance; for solo open-source, it's probably ceremony.

    Self-hosting on the dark web: this blog now lives as a Tor hidden service

    David Alvarez Rosa's writeup on moving his site behind a Tor .onion address: no certificate authority, no DNS, no exposed IP — the address derives from the public key and the connection is end-to-end encrypted by Tor itself. Includes the actual config: HiddenServiceDir/HiddenServicePort, the debian-tor ownership gotcha, reading the generated hostname. The pitch is practical, not ideological — including home hosting behind NAT with no static IP.

    • Why bother
      • Outgoing-only connections mean you can serve from home behind NAT with no port forwarding and no exposed IP
      • Tor's encryption is end-to-end by design — no CA trust involved
      • The network only works because people use it; running a relay helps
    • The Tor-specific engineering
      • Performance is its own discipline: inline everything (base64 images, inline CSS), prefer CSS animations, server-render, work without JS
      • Security becomes real and DDoS mitigation is Tor-specific — double captchas during attacks
      • Site fronting is the nasty one: anyone can proxy your site at a fake .onion address, and indistinguishable URLs make phishing easy; advertise via Onion-Location headers on your clearnet site
    • HN take
      • The old hands reminisce: one commenter's site has been Tor-hosted for years from home, wrote about it in 2600 a decade ago
      • The fronting debate eats half the thread — how do you prove which .onion is the real one without the normal web? Partial-address captchas, off-site publication of the address
    Source: david.alvarezrosa.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 271 pts / 97 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingPractical and nostalgic in equal measure. The most useful vein is the Tor-native performance engineering: everything inline, no JS dependence, server-side rendering — because the latency budget punishes every round trip differently than on the clearnet. The most heated vein is site fronting: critics point out that a malicious proxy at a different .onion can impersonate you and users can't tell the URLs apart, which arguably undermines the whole trust story; defenders counter with Onion-Location headers and off-site address publication. Nobody disputes the NAT-traversal benefit, which is the quiet killer feature — several commenters confirm home-hosted onion sites just work.

    In an $80 motel room, a discovery to shed light on the origin of plants

    Evolutionary biologist Julia Van Etten (U. of Maryland) turned a cheap Nags Head, N.C. motel room into a makeshift lab — brackish water jugs along the walls, bedside tables as microscope stands — and found two previously undescribed species of Paulinella (P. marae and P. murrayi). Published in the Journal of Phycology, they bring known photosynthetic Paulinella species from four to six — and Paulinella is one of only two known cases of a cell engulfing a photosynthetic bacterium, a live window into how plants got chloroplasts 1.5 billion years ago.

    • The discovery
      • Spotted initially in 2021 with a $300 hobby microscope on a family trip to the Outer Banks; follow-up fieldwork ran from the motel room
      • The team came together partly because of federal science funding freezes — scrappy science as necessity
      • Paulinella's second endosymbiosis (~100M years ago) is unreduced by time, unlike the ancient plant event — you can watch the early stages of the merger
    • The framing fight
      • Top comment: this has 'no relationship whatsoever' with the origins of life — it's the origin of plants, billions of years removed
      • Counter: it's 'related to the origins of life by abstraction' — a fundamental transition in life's history either way
      • Consensus: the science is interesting enough that it doesn't need the origins-of-life framing — NYT headline as clickbait
    • The lesson
      • 'The actual science is interesting enough that it doesn't really need the origins of life framing' — plus a win for low-cost fieldwork
    Source: nytimes.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 266 pts / 98 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is two stories at once: scientists geeking out about Paulinella's second endosymbiosis event, and a running pedantry war over the headline. The most-upvoted correction insists this is the origin of plants — a nucleated cell engulfing a cyanobacterium 1.5 billion years ago — not the origin of life, and that Paulinella's recent (~100M years) repeat of the trick is the actual story. The pushback is gentle: a fundamental transition in the history of life is at least adjacent to the headline's promise. The motel-room scrappiness itself gets near-universal affection, and the funding-freeze backstory lands as a quiet indictment.

    Replacing the old battery on rechargeable bike lights — a great first repair project

    Julia Evans' jvns.ca walkthrough of reviving rechargeable bike lights that died after a decade: they held charge for five minutes, the fix was replacing the internal Li-ion cell. Cut the silicone case, decode the battery code (third character R = rechargeable, '77' = height in tenths of a millimeter, measure diameter for the rest), desolder, solder in the new cell — at a community makerspace, with friends' help.

    • The technique
      • Button-cell designations encode everything: the R means rechargeable, the trailing digits are size in tenths of a millimeter
      • Desoldering is the crux skill — iFixit guides + a makerspace's soldering iron did it for a first-timer
      • No safety advice offered; the author is explicit she's not an expert — community space for help
    • HN take
      • The top comment decodes the code the author couldn't: third char R, '77' = height, measure diameter — a nice crowd-sourced correction
      • Warnings: AliExpress batteries lie about size ('1700mAh instead of the advertised 2600mAh'); resoldered cells may not sustain top power modes
      • Fenix lights take standard 18650/21700 cells — the manufacturer caveats are about cheap cells, not the form factor
    • The quality-of-life thread
      • Does anyone make a tail light with auto-shutoff? The answer: Garmin Varia lights shut off when they disconnect from the bike computer
    Source: jvns.ca · Discuss: HN Discuss · 188 pts / 105 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingClassic jvns thread: encouraging, practical, and immediately useful. The most-liked comment supplies the decoding rule the author explicitly asked for — how to read a battery designation without an LLM — which is the thread's emblem moment. The caveats come from the repair crowd: batteries from no-name sellers are routinely undersized, and a resoldered cell may behave subtly differently (a tail light's top mode flashing instead of steady). The Fenix digression — standard cells in bike lights, with the manufacturer's fine print decoded as 'don't use garbage batteries' — and the Garmin Varia auto-shutoff answer round out a thread that's basically a buyer-and-repair guide.
