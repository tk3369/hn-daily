    The dinner party is dying, and nobody can agree why

    Derek Thompson's essay on the decline of hosting — fewer dinner parties, a cultural drift away from being the person who throws them. Commenters sorted instantly into hosts and non-hosts, then argued about whether introversion is a trait or an excuse.

    • The claim
      • Hosting culture is measurably declining; fewer people throw dinner parties
      • The pattern reads as specifically American — European commenters report still-thriving party cultures
      • Personality sorts it: some are energized by hosting, others drained by it
    • HN take
      • Trait camp: introversion is a reproducible Big Five trait; the 80s had lone wolves too, they just couldn't say it
      • Taxonomy camp: the modern introvert/extrovert discourse is self-fulfilling — labels give people permission to opt out
      • The pragmatic read: 'there are people who throw parties and people who don't' was always true
    Source: derekthompson.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 305 pts / 248 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingConsensus: the decline is real, and personality sorts it — hosting energizes some people and drains others, and that's old news rather than a new crisis. The sharp disagreement is causal. One side says introversion is a stable, reproducible trait that always existed; the 80s simply weren't a culture where you could say it, citing left-handedness graphs as the analogy for reporting bias. The other side says the modern introvert/extrovert taxonomy is itself doing damage — introversion has become a socially acceptable excuse to hermit, and labeling a mood as an identity reinforces it. A middle thread suggests thinking of it as mood, not character.

    Wine demand is collapsing and California grape growers are stuck with the harvest

    California wine-grape farmers can't sell their harvest — demand for wine is in structural decline and there's nowhere to offload the grapes. HN reads this as a demand story, not a supply story: the cultural norms around alcohol have moved permanently, and growers hold a perishable asset with no buyers.

    • The shift
      • Lunch wines that were normal a decade ago now get you an HR trip
      • Offering beers at a family gathering draws looks 'as if you'd offered the kids drugs'
      • Nordic commenters add the policy dimension: state monopolies and punitive pricing worked as designed
    • HN take
      • Consensus: this isn't a bad vintage, it's a generational decline — structural, not cyclical
      • Denmark never had prohibitionist policies and demand fell there too, so culture is doing most of the work
      • The growers' problem has no obvious fix: grapes rot, and alternative uses don't pencil out at scale
    Source: kqed.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 287 pts / 719 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe consensus is blunt — this isn't a bad vintage, it's a generational shift in alcohol norms, and farmers are holding a perishable asset with no buyers. European commenters trace the change concretely: 10–15 years ago bankers drank wine at lunch; now being even slightly drunk around children is taboo. The policy subplot splits along Nordics-versus-Denmark lines: commenters from Finland, Norway, Sweden, and Iceland describe decades of state-run monopolies and high taxes doing exactly what they were designed to do — while Denmark never bothered with any of it, and demand declined there too, suggesting culture is doing most of the work and policy mostly accelerates it.

HN immediately attacked the denominator: 500k is 'two days of Liverpool Street' — either the cameras were off 99% of the time or the same faces are being counted daily.

    • The numbers
      • 16 deployments over 6 months yielded 500k scanned faces
      • Zero arrests, one false positive
      • 500k faces is two days of Liverpool Street's daily average — the scale doesn't add up
    • HN take
      • The article says faces, not people — repeats are likely being counted as scans
      • BTP says non-alert data is deleted immediately, so they can't know whether scans are repeats
      • The intent debate: fishing expedition vs. control system, with the Jan 6 retrospective identifications cited
    Source: theguardian.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 183 pts / 108 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe consensus is skepticism about the headline numbers — commenters note 500k faces is trivially small for 16 deployments over 6 months, and the article says faces, not people, so repeats are likely being counted. One careful thread parses the BTP claim that non-alert images and biometric data are 'automatically and immediately deleted' and notes the consequence: they therefore can't know whether scans are repeats, so the figure must be total scans, not unique faces. The disagreement is about intent: fishing expedition versus control system — with the use of similar systems to identify and arrest participants after the Capitol riot cited as evidence the technology's real value is retrospective identification, not real-time arrests.
