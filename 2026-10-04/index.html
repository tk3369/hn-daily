    Hard budget caps should be the default, not a warning email

    Simon Willison argues pay-by-usage services need default hard budget caps: after $X/month, cut the service off and return errors — soft 'we emailed you at midnight' warnings will not do, because agents now spin up billable code while you sleep. AWS launched project spend limits in September and Google Cloud added Spend Caps in July; Willison wants uncapped billing to become an explicit, scary opt-in.

    • The ask
      • Hard cap by default: exceed $X/month, service errors out
      • Removing the cap should be a prominent, explicit opt-in checkbox
      • Agents should steer new builders toward capped providers
    • Why now
      • Coding and personal agents lower the friction of creating billable services
      • Runaway loops can consume hundreds to thousands overnight
      • 'Businesses prefer errors to a surprise $10,000+ bill'
    • State of play
      • AWS: monthly spend limits for new projects (Sep 2026), still rolling out
      • Google Cloud: Spend Caps per service (July 2026)
      • HN check: GCP caps cover only a few services so far — 'it's fake' for most
    Source: simonwillison.net · Discuss: HN Discuss · 502 pts / 257 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingHN agrees with the direction and immediately audits the implementation. The top technical complaint is that Google Cloud's new caps, on inspection, apply to only a handful of services — 'fake' for the workloads people actually fear. The incentive problem gets stated plainly: why would a vendor make it harder to accidentally give them a million dollars, and a $10,000 surprise bill is 'getting off easy.' Several commenters go further than Willison — caps should be legally required, or billing without a negotiated contract should not exist at all — while others note the workaround already in use (virtual cards with hard limits) may be exactly what is pushing the clouds to build the real thing. One poster realized this morning that their new CI provider has no caps at all; fortunately, they say, not the hard way.

    Rodin Museum wins: 3D scans are not documents, court rules

    After eight years, France's Conseil d'État ruled against Cosmo Wenman's bid for the Rodin Museum's publicly funded 3D scans of public-domain sculptures: the scans, the court held, are legally indistinguishable from physical reproductions — part of the museum's inalienable collection, not administrative documents — so freedom-of-information law does not apply. The appeal was thrown out and Wenman ordered to pay the museum €3,000. A 2023 lower-court win on other scan formats was effectively undone.

    • The ruling
      • Conseil d'État: point-cloud scans are not administrative documents
      • Scans treated as collection items; FOI law held inapplicable
      • Appeal dismissed; €3,000 costs to the museum; earlier win undone
    • The record
      • 8-year case; CADA (the government's own FOI body) had sided with Wenman
      • Museum claimed scans were both unusable and a counterfeiting risk
      • Lower court in 2023 ordered some formats released; museum ignored the order
    • HN take
      • 'Establishment forces looking after each other to hold back data'
      • Counter-note: we hear one side; the museum's case is unargued here
      • Warning read: museums will simply stop making high-quality scans
    Source: cosmowenman.substack.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 243 pts / 116 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's first question is the right one: why did the museum fight this hard for eight years? The answers split. The cynical reading is revenue and control — high-quality scans let anyone produce reconstructions, ending a reproduction income stream — compounded by institutions protecting each other, with the court's own analyst supplying a surrealist 'sometimes a document is not a document' rationale the museum had not even argued. A dissenting voice insists on the missing other side and cautions that the account is one litigant's. The systemic warning gets the most traction: the lesson other museums will take is to never make high-quality scans in the first place, or never admit to having them — a preservation loss no ruling can undo. Whether an EU-level appeal exists is asked and, in-thread, unanswered.

    Agents don't need memory plugins. They need documentation.

    Kevin Liao argues the agent-memory plugin market — scrape transcripts into 1,000 vector snippets, inject the five most similar per prompt — solves the wrong problem: similarity is not correctness, snippets lose context, and the store is unauditable. His alternative is document-based memory: a plain-Markdown 'brain' (his open-source Operator Memory) that the agent consults before work and updates after, turning the loop from prompt → build → forget into prompt → consult → build → update.

    • The critique
      • Memory plugins: transcript snippets in a vector DB, top-5 injected per prompt
      • Similarity ranks embedding closeness, not truth, currency, or completeness
      • Unauditable: thousands of embeddings, no view of what is stale or wrong
    • The alternative
      • Structured Markdown workspace: instructions, specs, decisions, research
      • Agent reads relevant docs before work, updates them after
      • No embeddings, summarizers, or background 'dreamer' daemons; git-friendly
    • HN take
      • Naur's 'theory building' objection: docs never capture the full mental model
      • Token cost worry: reading and updating a growing brain balloons small tasks
      • 'Why install a tool? An AGENTS.md instruction plus a hook does this'
    Source: liao.gg · Discuss: HN Discuss · 242 pts / 132 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thesis lands; the product gets interrogated. Practitioners who have built similar systems warn that the read-and-update discipline consumes tokens fast as the document set grows, and that keeping stale pages from steering the agent is the real work. A thoughtful objection invokes Peter Naur's Programming as Theory Building — documentation alone cannot preserve the mental model behind a program — with the counter that agents, unlike humans, need far more of that model made explicit anyway. The minimalist camp is blunt: session history plus grep is already perfect recall for coding, and a memory layer is tokens spent building an imperfect copy next to the original. The middle position may be the consensus: memory as uncurated recall can harm as easily as help, while accurate, human-readable documentation is only ever beneficial — whether or not it ships as a plugin.

      • Recent 'rogue' incidents dismissed as not evidence of existential risk
      • Anthropic's Amodei named as the opposing camp
    • The landscape
      • LeCun's long-standing position: current architectures lack world models

    • HN take

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread splits into three risks and argues about which one is real. LeCun's defenders say the extinction frame anthropomorphizes a statistical tool, that his world-models critique has been consistent for years, and that the nearer dangers — state misuse, economic displacement — get crowded out by Terminator talk. The middle position concedes extinction and worries anyway: we can wipe ourselves out socially and economically with AI that never becomes an agent of anything. The skeptics of LeCun note that confidence is cheap and that dismissing incidents wholesale is its own kind of faith. Bill Gates gets invoked by both sides within a few comments of each other — as the man whose foresight built an industry, and as a nontechnical voice shouting that the Terminator is real. Nobody's mind visibly changes; the map of positions is the value.
