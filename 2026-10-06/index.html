    Cloudflare puts web search for agents behind one API

    Cloudflare's Web Search API is in beta: one call through AI Gateway lets agents search the live web instead of guessing URLs or relying on a training cutoff. At launch you pick Ceramic, Exa, or Linkup, billed at the provider's list price with no markup, or bring your own key.

    • The launch
      • Beta, via AI Gateway; REST API or Workers AI binding
      • Providers at launch: Ceramic, Exa, and Linkup
      • Billed at provider list price, no Cloudflare markup; BYO key allowed
    • The terms that matter
      • All three providers support Zero Data Retention via Cloudflare
      • All committed to Cloudflare's verified-bot crawling standards
      • Per-provider output terms still govern storing and sharing results
    • Why it matters
      • Search becomes a swappable commodity layer for agents
      • Gateway logs give one place to audit agent search calls
      • The provider choice, not the API shape, becomes the lock-in question
    Source: developers.cloudflare.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 570 pts / 266 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread mostly ignores Cloudflare and litigates one provider's terms instead. The top comment quotes Ceramic's prohibitions on storing, aggregating, or resyndicating search output, then argues the display exception is undercut by a clause barring output that is independently extractable by end users — hard to satisfy for anything visible. From there it splits: one camp calls it crawling the web and then forbidding anyone to crawl it back, the other points to a long body of copyright law distinguishing a database from its contents. The practical takeaway survives both: read the output terms before building an agent feature on any of these APIs.

The efficiency claims get interest; the generalization claim gets an audit.

    AI agents flag two room-temperature magnetic semiconductor candidates

    A Vals AI post says its agents used density-functional calculations to design one Luttinger-compensated magnet, YBaMnFeO5, and to spot the same property in KV[Cr(CN)6], a Prussian-blue relative first made in 1999. Both are predicted to have zero net magnetism yet spin-sorted band edges — useful for spintronics. These are calculations, not measurements: neither candidate's spin sorting has been measured yet.

    • Candidate 1: designed
      • YBaMnFeO5, never made; predicted 2.35 eV band gap
      • Predicted magnetic order to ~420-490 K in simulation
      • Catch: its required checkerboard ordering scrambles near synthesis temperatures
    • Candidate 2: found in 1999
      • KV[Cr(CN)6]; 1999 sample stayed ordered to 376 K
      • Predicted ~2.1 eV gap; large spin-sorted windows
      • Structure locks each metal to its own site — the thing candidate 1 lacks
    • Status
      • Predictions for perfect, dry crystals; the 1999 sample was a wet powder
      • Two simulation methods disagree on how much water weakens it
      • Next step stated in the post: remake it and measure the spin sorting
    Source: vals.ai · Discuss: HN Discuss · 408 pts / 274 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's first fight is about the primer, not the result: casting magnetism as a binary of ferromagnets and antiferromagnets ignores the diamagnets and paramagnets people actually meet, like copper and aluminium. That sloppiness feeds the second fight — several commenters suspect the post was LLM-written and insufficiently reviewed, and the thread veers into how much LLM text HN already carries undetected. The underlying claim gets less scrutiny than its packaging, which is itself the lesson: a computation-only candidate, presented in a shaky explainer, will be judged on the explainer.

    Example.com got a redesign — and broke people's workflows anyway

    DebugBear traces example.com's biggest change in decades: on Sep 28, IANA's reserved-domain page went multilingual, rotating through six languages every five seconds with a per-character fade, each letter wrapped in its own span. On Oct 3 the animation was removed and all languages now show at once. IANA says the split into a basic page plus a separate JavaScript file cuts bandwidth, because most traffic is automated and never fetches the JS.

    • What changed
      • Sep 28: rotating EN/AR/ZH/FR/RU/ES, per-character transition delays
      • Oct 3: animation removed; all languages shown from the start
      • An SVG book icon was also added in the redesign
    • IANA's reason
      • Bandwidth: automated traffic skips the JS file, so it is not served
      • The domain is for documentation examples, per the page itself
      • 'This is not a service; avoid relying on it for testing and monitoring'
    • The history
      • Reserved by IANA since the late 1990s for documentation
      • Earlier redesigns: 2013 (own page again, CDN), 2019 (fonts/copy), 2025 (new copy)
      • Now served via Cloudflare (since Dec 2025, per DebugBear)
    Source: debugbear.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 268 pts / 180 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingPeak HN: the top comment mourns a glasses-cleaning workflow — the old near-white page was ideal for spotting smudges, and the new dark-by-default page ruins it, xkcd 1172 duly linked. The substantive split is over testing: people admit their suites and connectivity checks lean on example.com, others quote the page back at them — it explicitly says it is not a service and not to rely on it for testing or monitoring — and suggest captive-portal endpoints or infrastructure you control instead.     Flatten SF: the flattest route between any two points, in your browser

    Flatten SF computes routes across San Francisco in the browser over 160,000 street segments, using USGS 1-metre lidar elevation and the Overture/OpenStreetMap street graph. A slider walks the Pareto frontier from shortest to flattest — where a foot of climb is priced at 200 feet of walking — using cumulative gain, not endpoint difference. Place search is offline, built into the page.

    • How it works
      • 160K street segments; USGS 1 m lidar elevation
      • Slider: every route nothing else beats on both distance and climb
      • 1 ft of climb = 200 ft of walking at the flat extreme
    • Details that matter
      • Climbing is cumulative gain, not net endpoint difference
      • Stairways allowed on foot, excluded on a bike
      • Offline search: intersections, places, addresses built into the page
    • By
      • Drew Edwards; source, data, and full analysis on GitHub
      • No server routing — computation happens in your browser
      • Faint lines show the other Pareto routes as you slide

    Nobel Prize in Physics goes to Francis Halzen for IceCube neutrinos

    The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded solely to Francis Halzen 'for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin.' IceCube is a cubic-kilometre detector buried in Antarctic ice; it catches neutrinos via the Cherenkov light from the charged particles they create in the ice.

    • The prize
      • Francis Halzen, sole laureate (prize share 1/1)
      • Citation: IceCube contributions + high-energy astrophysical neutrinos
      • Announced during Nobel week, Oct 5-12 announcements
    • The instrument
      • Cubic-kilometre detector in Antarctic ice
      • Neutrinos convert to charged particles in the ice
      • Detected via Cherenkov radiation (faster than light in the medium, not in vacuum)
    • Why it matters
      • High-energy neutrinos point back to cosmic sources light cannot escape
      • A decades-long bet on an extreme-environment instrument
      • Neutrino astronomy as a working field, not a proposal
    Source: nobelprize.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 243 pts / 75 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingHN's physicists explain the Cherenkov mechanism clearly, and then the thread becomes a logistics appreciation post. Building a cubic kilometre of instrument at the literal South Pole — the furthest possible end of any supply chain, as one commenter puts it, proposing it as a refined synonym for the middle of nowhere — runs through the South Pole Traverse, air cargo at great cost, and diesel generators running it year-round.     Dust: pretraining transformers without backpropagation

    Q Labs' Dust is a zeroth-order method that perturbs activations — independently at every token — so one forward pass evaluates a whole virtual population in parallel, instead of materialising perturbed weight copies. The authors claim it is the first such method competitive with backprop at pretraining transformers, exceeding it in some settings at large populations, and orders of magnitude more efficient than weight-space evolution strategies. They also state the limit plainly: not compute-efficient enough to replace backprop today.

    • The method
      • Node perturbation: Gaussian noise on layer outputs, per token
      • Reward-weighted noise estimates the gradient; no chain rule
      • Each token is a virtual population member — one pass evaluates them all
    • The claims
      • Competitive with backprop at large populations; exceeds it in some settings
      • Claimed 1,000-10,000x more efficient than EGGROLL (extrapolated)
      • Larger models were more population-efficient, up to 1B tokens tested
    • The stated limits
      • Approximates backprop at substantially more compute
      • Authors: not a backprop replacement today
      • The prize is architectures backprop cannot train at all
    Source: qlabs.sh · Discuss: HN Discuss · 236 pts / 62 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe top commenter would bet their life savings that no derivative-free method ever makes an impact: neural objectives are smooth, gradients tell you where to go, and the advantage grows with parameter count — with a strict complexity gap between gradient-based and derivative-free optimisation cited, and a correction that the gap has been textbook material since Nesterov rather than a new result. The defence is biological and pragmatic: brains almost certainly do not run full backprop and train a PhD on far less energy, and for genuinely discontinuous objectives gradient-like methods still need help. Interesting, well-argued, and — on the thread's own evidence — not close to settled in Dust's favour.
