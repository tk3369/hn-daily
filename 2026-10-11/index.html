    2D Vehicles: the 1996 weekend prototype that became GTA's car physics, remastered in JavaScript for its 30th year

    Pat Kerr recreates, in JavaScript, the 2D physics simulation he wrote in one weekend in late August 1996 in GFA BASIC on an Atari ST — the prototype that later became the vehicle system in Grand Theft Auto. The remaster is playable in the browser with three modes (Car, the simpler Ship, and Brick), toggleable collision barriers and gravity, and the same camera tricks the game used: a dead zone, and a speed-related zoom that pulls back as you go faster.

    • How it works
      • Two layers: a general-purpose 2D rigid-body simulation, plus a deliberately simple car model on top
      • It started as the Brick, gained Thrust-like Ship elements, then the additions that made it feel like a car
      • Kerr notes rigid-body vehicle physics was uncommon in games at the time; most used basic point physics
    • The recreation
      • Playable in the browser, with touch controls on mobile; keys 1/2/3 switch modes, B toggles barriers, G gravity, Z/X camera
      • Framed explicitly as independent and unaffiliated with GTA, Rockstar or Take-Two
    • Why it matters
      • A working artefact of how a genre-defining game started: one weekend prototype, not a design document
      • Prototyping as a toy, with no production baggage, is what made the feel discoverable
    Source: patkerr.co.uk · Discuss: HN Discuss · 529 pts / 106 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingAffection first: players say it still feels great and recognisably like the original, and one credits it with teaching them handbrake turns — until tyre costs cured the habit. The top comment pushes back on the remaster itself, asking why a programmer of Kerr's talent would revisit old work with modern tooling instead of pushing forward; others answer that the history is the point, and that isolated toy prototypes are how concepts like this get found. A side thread compares alternative vehicle models, including a bicycle-model parking solver.

    The Lightbulb Computer: a projector and camera in a lightbulb socket, instead of a headset on your face

    Guillaume Ardaud's speculative design fits a projector and computer vision into a large lightbulb that screws into a standard Edison socket or sits in a portable lamp base. It reacts to voice, sees where you point, analyses what it sees, and projects content onto real surfaces — recipe widgets and timers in the kitchen, answers next to the book you are reading, a shared map for trip planning. The pitch is ambient and shared: information in the room, not on a small screen or piped through glasses.

    • The form factor
      • Ceiling mounting gives a vantage over the whole room and can project anywhere in it
      • Edison sockets are a near-ubiquitous worldwide standard; a battery base makes it portable
      • Positioned against smart glasses: no constraining, socially awkward device on your face
    • What it does (design prototypes)
      • Voice commands plus pointing: it watches where you point and responds there
      • Kitchen overlays for mise en place, study Q&A beside the page, shared maps with gesture pan and scroll
      • A series of design prototypes exploring the direction, not a shipping product
    • Why it matters
      • Tests whether spatial computing needs a wearable at all, if the room itself can be the display
      • Shared projection solves the XR problem of everyone else seeing a weird, private object
    Source: lightbulbcomputer.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 491 pts / 72 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe idea lands. Several commenters compare the feeling to the first pre-iPhone multitouch demos, and the Humane Pin is invoked kindly for once: a stationary, ambient version solves many of its interaction and engineering problems. The sharpest suggestion is latency: timestamp the word 'here' in speech and analyse the buffered frame from that moment, so the user does not have to hold a pointing pose while the computer catches up — ideally with a projected dot confirming the capture. One sceptic asks whether the demos are a real working prototype or animation; the rest mostly want one.

    Discretionary Review: a San Francisco housing simulator where every rule and vote comes from the public record

    This browser game puts you through San Francisco's discretionary-review process for housing: height limits, supervisor districts, homeowner and coalition politics, and project approvals, simulated from January 2015 onward. The in-game rulebook states that every rule, vote count and case is drawn from the public record, with sources in the project's research docs, while neighbourhood-politics scores and the economics are simplified for play. The city, as the HN title puts it, would prefer you didn't build.

    • The simulation
      • Projects move through sites, height limits and district politics; a right-hand panel scores neighbourhood reaction
      • An in-game newspaper, the Fog City Ledger, reports as you play
      • Rules, vote counts and cases sourced from the public record; politics and economics simplified, and labelled as such
    • Why it matters
      • Makes an opaque approval process legible by making you operate it
      • The friction is the content: the process itself is the argument
    Source: housing.over.pizza · Discuss: HN Discuss · 412 pts / 137 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingPlayers split on the interface before the politics: several find the UI complex, convoluted and verbose, and ask who it is for and why learning it is worth the time, while others praise the aesthetics and say it took them back to SimFarm.     Build your own decision model: one forward pass, masked tokens, calibrated probabilities — with Qwen3-1.7B

    Nish Tahir walks through building a Jev-style decision model yourself. The trick: constrain the output to a fixed set of option tokens, mask everything else in the vocabulary, and read the answer off a single forward pass instead of generating token by token — the worked example takes 11 passes for structured JSON where the decision model takes one. The post is equally clear on the caveat: raw token probabilities measure next-token confidence, not correctness, so calibration — adjusting temperature against a benchmark — is part of the build, using Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B.

    • The mechanism
      • Fixed options only: mask the vocabulary so the model can only emit an allowed option token
      • Select the highest-probability option; one pass replaces multi-token generation
      • Worked code uses Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B via transformers, with an interactive in-page demo
    • The caveat
      • Uncalibrated token probabilities are not true probabilities of being right
      • Calibration is presented as required work, not an optional refinement
    • Why it matters
      • Decision models are a learnable technique, not a proprietary product: the core is masking plus calibration
      • Cheap, fast classification is the layer routing, gating and screening systems are built on
    Source: nishtahir.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 378 pts / 83 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingReaders who work in ML greet it with a familiar frustration: classifiers have been hard to sell for years, plain LLMs already work as zero-shot classifiers, and the hype around Jev makes one MLE 'scream internally' — while conceding zero-shot convenience is real. The calibration step draws the methodological challenge: is fitting temperature to a benchmark after the fact legitimate calibration or p-hacking? Others simply enjoy playing the token generation interactively, and a lighter CPU alternative, Jeffy, is linked for people who want to train their own.

    Cloudflare Radar: bot traffic from Yandex's AS13238 fell to near zero after strikes on its data centres

    Cloudflare Radar data for AS13238 (Yandex LLC) shows a sudden drop in bot traffic — requests Radar scores 1–29, i.e. likely automated — following strikes on Yandex data centres. Commenters tracking the chart report HTTP traffic from Yandex down to roughly 0% after a third strike, after the first two had already degraded it, while Yandex's search pages still serve. Read the chart for what it is: one network's view of one autonomous system's automated requests, not a measure of Yandex as a whole.

    • What the data shows
      • Radar classifies bot vs human by bot score: 1–29 likely automated, 30+ likely human, on HTML requests by default
      • The linked view is AS13238 over a 7-day range; the drop follows the data-centre strikes
      • Crawler traffic is the visible casualty; search serving continued, per thread reports
    • Why it matters
      • Indexing is deferrable work: lose capacity and bot traffic falls faster than user traffic
      • Physical strikes now show up, measurably and publicly, in internet telemetry within days
    Source: radar.cloudflare.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 355 pts / 135 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread supplies the missing definitions and the missing scale: one commenter posts Radar's bot-score glossary, another notes Yandex is 'Russian Google' — of course it emits bot traffic, that is what indexing is, and Google taken offline would show the same shape. Several track the decline strike by strike and link reporting on cascading outages across Yandex Go, Pay, food delivery and smart-home services as failover broke down after three facilities were hit in four days. The jokes about donating to the Ukrainian military sit alongside a plainer argument that 'AI agents' is a rebranding of bots.

    Show HN: Talorys — a personal AI agent running entirely in your own Cloudflare account, free-tier friendly

    Talorys is an open-source, single-user personal AI agent that deploys into your own Cloudflare account with one command (npx create-talorys@latest). It runs on Workers, Workers AI, Durable Objects and Pages, and provides AI chat with persistent memory and conversation history, task, note and project management, and scheduled reminders and recurring automations, behind password-protected access with no signup and no telemetry. The author's stated goal: an assistant they own and control, with no monthly subscription and no one else's hosted service.

    • The stack
      • Cloudflare Workers, Workers AI, Durable Objects and Pages — the whole agent lives in your account
      • One-command deploy; free-tier friendly; single-user, password-protected, no telemetry
      • Chat with persistent memory, tasks, notes, projects, reminders and recurring automations
    • The catch, from the thread
      • Workers AI free tier is metered in 'neurons' — 10,000 a day — a unit commenters find opaque
      • One user warns of confusing neuron billing on paid Workers plans and an ignored support ticket
    • Why it matters
      • The ownership model is the feature: your account, your data, commodity infrastructure
      • It also shows the limit of that model — you own the agent, Cloudflare owns the ground it stands on
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 304 pts / 140 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe title takes the beating: 'self-hosted' is the thread's oxymoron of the day — several commenters define self-hosted as something you host yourself, and relying on Cloudflare for literally everything does not meet it. The author does not really dispute the infrastructure point; the claim is ownership and control versus a subscription service. Practical warnings follow: the free Workers AI tier's 'neurons' unit baffles people, and one user reports being billed for neuron usage they believed was covered, with a support ticket ignored — a known issue, they say, in Cloudflare's support Discord.

    Nix wrote half of my debugger: Rewind's hard parts — inputs, symbols, sources, reproducibility — were already derivations

    Farid Zakaria's Rewind VM makes every Nix build run a pure function of its inputs, thread schedule included, and he has been using it to find and reproduce race conditions in Nix builds. As he added debugger features — a source panel, stack frames, a Compare tab, gdb support, thread lanes showing who held the CPU at each step, and 'Check from here' — each one turned out to need work Nix had already done: a debugger needs a program's exact inputs, debug symbols, its sources and every library's sources, reproducible on someone else's machine. That is exactly what a derivation is.

    • The demonstration
      • A two-teller bank-account race lost money in 396 of 1,000 runs on a 16-core laptop, and in none of 1,000 pinned to one core
      • Rewind's single-CPU VM passes on the first run too, so rewind check re-runs under perturbed schedules and narrows the first failure to one step
      • Thread lanes show who held the CPU at every step; 'Check from here' finds the exact step where a race begins
    • The claim
      • Each debugger feature's hard part — inputs, symbols, sources, reproducibility — was already solved by Nix derivations
      • Deterministic builds are not just hygiene; they are the substrate a real debugger can stand on
    • Why it matters
      • Reproducibility compounds: one property, paid once, keeps supplying tools that are expensive to build any other way
    Source: fzakaria.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 215 pts / 51 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingAdmiring, with one structural question: a commenter asks what Nix has to do with it, since Rewind looks like reimplementing rr — the answer in the post is that the derivation system supplies the inputs and symbols rr-style tools must assemble themselves. Others generalise the move: more and more non-build work is being pushed into Nix builds to avoid hand-rolling cache invalidation. A recurring prediction is that AI assistance accelerates Nix adoption — many developers understand what Nix offers but would not master it unaided — set against one user's story of a complex flake in which Nix deleted itself, and trust went with it.

    Knuth reward check: the error was on page 1, in the first word — and the check, 0x$1.20, is framed twenty years on

    Thomas Hühn recounts receiving a Knuth reward check twenty years ago for an error in Computer Modern Typefaces — Volume E of Computers and Typesetting, which is why the memo field says 'E1': page 1. The error sits in the first paragraph, in the very first word, in a less-read book than the TeXbook or The Art of Computer Programming, and had been read over by practically everyone who opened it. He waited months and re-checked before reporting it. The check reads 0x$1.20 — hexadecimal dollars, $2.88 — and a later, wrong, error report still earned $0.32, because Knuth counted a throwaway sentence in it as a good suggestion and wrote paragraphs explaining why the main claim was wrong.

    • The find
      • Error in Computer Modern Typefaces, page 1, first paragraph, first word; memo 'E1' = Volume E, page 1
      • Reported only after months of re-checking; a local university expert dismissed the excitement
      • Knuth confirmed the error and sent the check; Hühn has censored the check image, as Knuth no longer issues real checks
    • The money, precisely
      • First check 0x$1.20 = $2.88; a later wrong report earned $0.32 for a good suggestion buried in it
      • Modern recipients get a certificate from the fictional Bank of San Serriffe instead of a real check
    • Why it matters
      • Folklore, verified in one case: the reward is real, small, and earned by reading page 1 carefully
      • The $0.32 check is the better story — being wrong carefully still contributed
    Source: thomas-huehn.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 207 pts / 75 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingA collectors' thread: several commenters have checks of their own — one has two, another knows someone with four or more, and one confesses the biggest shame of his life is having had one and lost it. The technical discussion picks at the error itself: the claim said infinitely many alphabets can be generated, and a commenter notes there are finitely many parameters with finite ranges, so 'a humongous number' would have been correct. Practical note for readers: the images failed to load for some, and a Wayback Machine copy is linked in the thread.
