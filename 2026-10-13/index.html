    Hackers trigger defrost across European supermarket fridges — deliveries and store prices hit too

    Tom's Hardware reports an attack in which hackers activated automatic defrosting in supermarket fridge systems across Europe, spoiling stock; deliveries and in-store prices were also impacted. The vulnerable part is the control path: commercial refrigeration controllers are sold on remote diagnosis and control — start a defrost without a callout, compare performance across a whole estate — which is exactly the access an attacker needs to start defrosts everywhere at once.

    • The attack
      • Automatic defrosting activated remotely across supermarket fridges in Europe; frozen and chilled stock spoiled, deliveries and store prices impacted
      • Vendor pitch for the same connectivity: remote diagnosis, remote defrost, estate-wide efficiency comparison
    • Why it matters
      • Monitoring and control were bundled: a system that only reported temperatures could not be told to defrost
      • This is the IoT pattern at infrastructure scale — a convenience feature becomes a single point of failure for a food supply chain
    Source: tomshardware.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 789 pts / 910 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingAt 910 comments this is the day's biggest thread, and its first move is to strike the word 'unprecedented': commenters link an August 2026 incident in which US military commissary freezers were all put into defrost mode and the food spoiled — the same attack, a month earlier, as a warning nobody acted on. The main argument is architectural: why is a defrost control reachable at all, when remote monitoring could be read-only and a human has to visit the fridge to unload it anyway? Defenders of connectivity cite failure alerts and energy optimisation; the counter is that none of that requires giving the network write access to the cooling system.

    A modern web app in C: one CGI binary, SQLite, Stripe payments — shipped as a real commercial product

    Corey Prophitt shipped marketyour.games — accounts, a creator search dashboard, folders, Stripe payments, Postmark email, no JavaScript required — as a C program served by CGI, the 1994 model. The stack is BCHS: OpenBSD, C, httpd, SQLite. Every request spawns a fresh process; deploying is replacing one file, with no restart and no downtime. Three dependencies: libcurl, libsodium, and vendored single-file SQLite and cJSON. A clean build takes seconds.

    • The stack
      • No framework, no templating language, no database server: C reads CGI variables, writes HTML directly, opens SQLite through its C API
      • OpenBSD defaults do security work: httpd serves from a chroot, pledge and unveil sandbox the binary
    • Why it matters
      • The whole system fits in one head — the measured trade is developer comfort for a dependency and operations count near zero
      • CGI's 2004 verdict is being re-litigated: per-request process isolation is a feature when machines are fast and deploys are file copies
    Source: prophitt.me · Discuss: HN Discuss · 632 pts / 277 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingAdmiration, with the obvious objection stated plainly: most of the listed benefits — one binary, SQLite, minimal dependencies — also apply to Go or Rust, with memory safety included, so why accept C's string handling for HTML output? Systems commenters work the details: fork-per-request costs milliseconds that FastCGI was invented to avoid, and SQLite's write behaviour under concurrent CGI processes gets questioned. A quieter strand simply agrees with the shape — modern PHP users report building the same way, frameworks only when a large team forces one.

    GLM-Legal: Z.ai takes the GLM line into legal work, where a hallucinated case is a sanction, not a typo

    Z.ai's blog announces GLM-Legal, a legal-domain entry in its GLM model line. The workflow shown leans on tracked changes and human lawyer review rather than autonomous output — the only defensible shape in a field where the known failure mode is specific and public: LLMs inventing cases and holdings, filed by lawyers who did not check, ending in sanctions. The examples shown focus on common-law work, the US and UK being where the legal industry's money is.

    • The product
      • Legal-domain GLM from Z.ai (z.ai blog); presented around drafting and review workflows with human sign-off
      • Not presented as an open model — notable as Chinese labs trend toward keeping domain models proprietary
    • Why it matters
      • Legal is the vertical where hallucination has a documented cost: judges have already sanctioned filings with invented citations
      • The open question is liability: when the model is wrong and the human missed it, the current answer is that the lawyer pays
    Source: z.ai · Discuss: HN Discuss · 615 pts / 342 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread goes straight to accountability: several commenters want disclosure laws for attorneys' AI use, on the grounds that a client should not learn from a sanctions order that their filing was prompt output. Others note the workflow defence — tracked changes plus human review — only works if the human actually verifies citations, which is precisely the step that failed in the publicised cases. A colder take predicts the economics: if a human sign-off remains legally required, lawyers keep high-paid jobs while models do the heavy lifting underneath.

    AI is a tool and using it well takes skill: a direct rebuttal to Rich Hickey's 'AI is not a tool'

    Huahai Yang answers Rich Hickey's October 11 essay, which argued LLMs are automation, not tools, because they cannot be wielded. The rebuttal: a saw does not guarantee a straight cut and a chisel does not make a sculptor — tools amplify poor judgement as readily as good, and usefulness depends on directing the work, reading feedback and correcting mistakes. Automation does not disqualify a tool either: a compiler and a spreadsheet both do substantial work unsupervised, and we still wield them.

    • The argument
      • Unpredictability is a quality by which tools are judged, not a disqualifier from the category
      • The claimed LLM workflow — ask, challenge the explanation, inspect the source, test, reject, refine — is a human-directed feedback loop
    • Why it matters
      • Framing decides practice: 'tool' implies operator skill and responsibility; 'automation' implies output you accept
      • If skill is real, it should be specifiable — which is exactly where the thread pushes back
    Source: yyhh.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 485 pts / 393 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe strongest pushback is a demand for receipts: name the skill concretely, in a form others can replicate — a saw technique can be taught to 100% reliability, while a compiler gives the same output for the same input and an LLM does not. Practitioners split by experience: one camp says the prompt-craft skills of mid-2025 are already obsolete and what remains is knowing what to ask for; another says AI used to learn a codebase is a skill amplifier, while most users treat it as a wishing machine and outsource the thinking. Several call the whole framing debate one of HN's least productive genres.

    A WiFi 7 router becomes a 320 Msps software-defined radio receiver

    A GitHub project (h0m3us3r, w1700k-sdr) turns a WiFi 7 router into a 320 megasample-per-second SDR receiver — commodity networking silicon re-purposed as radio test gear. The same developer previously built an SDR from an ESP32 Wi-Fi microcontroller. At 5 GHz, commenters note, there is no cheaper known way to get a receiver with this sample rate; dedicated SDR hardware at these rates is a different price class entirely.

    • The hack
      • WiFi 7 router (W1700K chipset) driven as a 320 Msps SDR receiver; code on GitHub
      • Same author's earlier work: an ESP32 turned into an SDR (eSpDR)
    • Why it matters
      • Every mass-produced Wi-Fi chipset is a wideband radio with a locked door; this is another door opened
      • Cheap receivers change who can experiment: spectrum work that needed lab budgets moves to a router's price
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 383 pts / 104 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingRadio people are genuinely impressed, with one deflationary correction: a Wi-Fi chipset already is an SDR, so the feat is access, not transmutation. Practical questions dominate — what is the SNR, can it be had in Europe, and will it work on the Banana Pi R4, which shares the chipset and adds PCIe/NVMe? The most ambitious reply sketches the endpoint: add attenuators, filters and a mixer stage and the router becomes the core of a small, high-performance service monitor good to 5 GHz — lab gear assembled from consumer parts.

    11-year-old unsoundness bugs found in Rust's borrow checker — and the minimal example is ordinary code

    A Rust compiler issue reports unsoundness bugs in the borrow checker that have sat there for 11 years. What alarms the thread is not exoticism but the opposite: the minimal example is short, plausible code — a generic wrapper with a 'static bound and a Drop implementation that ends up holding a reference which has outlived its data. Variance is the mechanism, and variance is the part of the type system almost nobody writes or tests by hand.

    • The bug
      • Unsoundness in the borrow checker, present for 11 years, filed as rust-lang/rust issue #164250
      • Minimal reproduction is simple, normal-looking code — not a contrived edge case
    • Why it matters
      • Rust's core promise is that safe code cannot do this; an 11-year hole in the checker is a hole in the guarantee itself
      • It raises the audit question for certified variants: whether the Ferrocene safety-critical compiler shares the issue
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 343 pts / 173 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingRust developers react with disbelief at the reproduction's simplicity — this is code shape you meet in normal work, and several say it took minutes to even see the trick, which is itself the explanation for 11 undiscovered years: hand-written variance is rare, so the tests never covered it. Context arrives quickly: collections of known Rust soundness bugs exist, a paper cataloguing them was posted to HN before to little attention, and the general verdict is type systems are hard — Rust is better, never perfect, and its safety claims may need the disclaimer printed.

    The baffling intelligence of a single cell: E. coli navigates by comparing now with a few seconds ago (2024)

    James Somers' interactive essay walks through E. coli chemotaxis: a bacterium with no brain swims toward food by storing its recent chemical past — in chemical modifications to its own proteins — and comparing the present against a few seconds ago, biasing its tumble-and-run movement accordingly. Memory, comparison and decision, implemented in chemistry. The piece is from 2024; it is back on the front page on the strength of the interactive presentation as much as the biology.

    • The mechanism
      • State stored as chemical modifications; the cell compares current nutrient concentration with its seconds-old record and adjusts turning behaviour
      • An interactive, animated treatment (jsomers.net) of a classic molecular-biology system
    • Why it matters
      • It is the minimal working example of navigation: sensing plus memory plus a comparator, with no neurons anywhere
      • A live design question for AI: real neurons have properties artificial ones lack, and single cells already compute more than the neuron model suggests
    Source: jsomers.net · Discuss: HN Discuss · 340 pts / 95 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread argues about the word in the title. One side: a nutrient sensor that raises concentration readings and flips turning direction below a threshold is a fully automatic mechanism, the opposite of intelligence. The other side treats that as the point — memory-and-compare is the same computational primitive a neuron uses, slower and simpler, and a single cell resembles a small neural network more than a single artificial neuron. In between, pure craft appreciation: multiple commenters call it one of the best-designed pages they have seen, and a previous 2024 discussion is linked for the longer argument.

    AI models can provably hide arbitrary capabilities: encrypted circuits in the weights that only a secret trigger unlocks

    A NeurIPS 2026 paper (arXiv 2610.13928) constructs backdoored models that embed an encrypted neural network inside the host model's weights, decrypting and executing it only on a secret trigger. Using digital lockers, the hidden capability is provably hard to elicit or interpret under cryptographic assumptions — even with full access to the weights. The PyTorch proof of concept, tested on a chemical-reaction prediction task as a CBRN proxy, resisted fine-tuning, weight noising, gradient-based trigger search and latent adversarial training.

    • The result
      • Extends prior work from hiding fixed strings to hiding arbitrary computations inside model weights
      • Capability audits assume hidden behaviour can be elicited; this is a constructive counterexample under adversarial conditions
    • Why it matters
      • Elicitation-based evaluation has a provable limit — a clean audit cannot, in general, certify that a capability is absent
      • The authors release the models to support defences; the immediate use is as a test case for audit methods
    Source: arxiv.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 245 pts / 115 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread separates the theorem from the threat model. One camp calls it a cool but deflationary demo: the construction follows from Rice's-theorem-style limits, and today's hidden weights are detectable in practice. The reply is that proofs of concept are the point — no known theoretical barrier stops a future version being indistinguishable from normal weights, whether planted by an attacker or learned. Practical consequences get named: sleeper behaviour in free open-weight models is the standing worry, and fully inspectable training data becomes a stronger selling point for open providers.
