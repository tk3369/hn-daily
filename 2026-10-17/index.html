    Tesla Roadster returns: drives upside down, flips with SpaceX thrusters, production now 2028

    Tesla unveiled a redesigned Roadster at SpaceX's McGregor, Texas test site on Oct 15, developed with SpaceX. In the demo the white two-seater drove upside down on a ramp using an underbody suction system, turned in the air and landed using built-in compressed-air attitude thrusters, then jumped over a truck and other vehicles while twisting. Tesla says 0-60 mph in under one second (a live run was claimed at 0.81s) and a top speed above 230 mph. Production is now slated for 2028, more than a decade after the next-generation Roadster was first shown in 2017. No target price was announced at the event; the original 2017 quote was $200,000 base. Performance figures are Tesla's claims for a prototype, not independently verified production results.

    • The demo
      • Suction holds the car inverted on a ramp; cold-gas thrusters, powered by compressed air with no fuel, flip it and can also boost forward acceleration
      • Musk cautioned before the demo that success was not guaranteed; the car shown was a prototype on a closed course
    • Why it matters
      • Tesla has no halo car today; the Roadster is the showpiece, while Musk himself has called it financially a 'small potato' next to autonomy and robotics
      • Road legality of the stunt features was not clarified at the event — spectacle confirmed, product still to be proven in 2028

    Claude reveals ECDSA curve origins: NIST seeds were lrand48 output seeded by Unix timestamps

    Anthropic reports that Claude, in under half an hour in October 2026, solved a decades-old cryptography mystery: where the seed values for the NIST ECDSA curves (P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384, P-521) came from. The seeds were provided by the NSA with no explanation, which fuelled years of backdoor speculation, a bounty, and the folk story that they were hashes of a humorous passphrase like 'Give Bob and Jerry a raise'. Anthropic says none of them were hashes at all: they are output of the C library's lrand48 generator seeded with a Unix timestamp, sampled 20 bytes at a time — P-256, for example, took 1,842 iterations to reach the standardized seed. Anthropic is explicit: this is not a break or a weakness, and no action is needed.

    • The finding
      • Each seed is reproducible from a timestamp plus an iteration count — independently verifiable, which is the point
      • The late Jerry Solinas's passphrase story, repeated by several colleagues, turned out to be wrong; Anthropic staff had chased that clue unsuccessfully first
    • Why it matters
      • The unexplained provenance was the main factual hook for P-256 backdoor theories; a boring, checkable origin removes it
      • It does not retroactively settle trust in other standards — Dual_EC_DRBG remains the documented counterexample in-thread
    Source: anthropic.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 931 pts / 340 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingRelief, jokes and residual suspicion. The top line — '13 years of tinfoil hats for srand48(time(NULL))' — gets corrected in detail: the timestamps span almost a year, clustering in August 1997, so what those dates meant is still an open, probably boring question. Several commenters ask why NIST burned so much trust instead of explaining this years ago; the counter-argument is that the people involved genuinely did not remember. Dual_EC_DRBG is invoked repeatedly as the reason suspicion was rational, and one commenter notes the practical upside: Bitcoin and Ethereum can now adopt P-256 signature checks without the seed-provenance worry.

    90% of US working hours are tasks AI benchmarks barely cover, Mercor maps 923 occupations

    Mercor Research built a Benchmark Map of the US economy: 923 O*NET occupations in 22 job families, 18,797 tasks, checked against an index of 924 AI benchmarks. The headline result: only 208 of 923 occupations have even one task covered by at least five benchmark prompts, six of 22 job families have no such occupation, and by Mercor's estimate 90% of American working hours are spent on tasks with little or no benchmark coverage. Coverage is not random: benchmarks concentrate on large, high-wage, AI-exposed markets first — lawyers' tasks are heavily covered while similarly exposed legal secretaries and court clerks are barely covered, leaving a long tail of middle-income knowledge work unmeasured.

    • The method
      • A single benchmark prompt can map to multiple O*NET tasks; coverage probability is modelled against AI-exposure tags and occupation wage bill (BLS wage and headcount, 40-hour week)
      • Mercor presents this as a first, continuously updated map, not a final census — the benchmark index will grow
    • Why it matters
      • If benchmarks do not cover the work, benchmark scores cannot tell you how models perform at the work Americans actually do
      • The gap is a measurement problem before it is a capability claim, in either direction
    Source: mercor.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 546 pts / 477 comments

    How the Claude Code team does code review: machines read first, humans spend attention last

    The Claude Code team describes its review process now that Claude writes about 90% of Claude Code's own code. From June to September, merged pull requests to that codebase more than doubled while the share that had to be reverted fell by more than 40%. The model: review runs continuously, like spellcheck, instead of as a gate at the end — most changes are reviewed and fixed by Claude at least once before a human looks — and the team optimizes explicitly for the minimum human review time that does not raise reverts or incidents. Context: in Anthropic's monorepo, changes landing per day went from about 2,500 in May to more than 30,000 in September. Ownership stays human: 'you land it, you own it.'

    • The principle
      • Replace 'did a human read the code?' with 'what could go wrong with this change, and which check — automated or human — is the right one to catch it?'
      • Cutting human reading time only counts if the time does not reappear as fixing what bad code breaks
    • Why it matters
      • A worked example, with revert numbers, of review redesigned for agent-volume code rather than human-volume code
      • Review's second job — building shared codebase understanding across the team — is acknowledged as the harder one to automate
    Source: claude.dev · Discuss: HN Discuss · 398 pts / 376 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingPractitioners trade war stories more than they dispute the model. One links the approach to a public repo carrying about 5,000 issues, a third of them regressions of previous behaviour; others dig into Claude Code harness specifics — manually invoked skills needing to be first in the prompt, arguments injected twice, /loop re-running at max context without caching. The sceptical note is volume: issue trackers filling with AI-generated reports and fixes faster than humans can triage them, which is either the problem this process solves or the problem it creates, depending on the commenter.

    You could have invented transformers from memory: attention as associative recall, step by step

    Alex Wang's essay derives the transformer starting from associative memory — a store that recalls a value when queried with a similar key. Start with the basic linear store, which accumulates key-value pairs as outer products and recalls by similarity; its capacity is the problem. Each fix raises capacity: with 64-dimensional keys, the essay walks from about 64 memories, to about 2,700, to over a million, and the endpoint of that progression is softmax attention. The framing also covers why a gradient-descent step on a linear layer is itself a memory write — the weight update is an outer product of input and error signal — connecting training and attention as two views of the same mechanism.

    • The arc
      • Memory store, capacity limits, then successive fixes — attention falls out as the high-capacity associative recall mechanism, not as an arbitrary invention
      • Builds on the 'Birth of a Transformer: A Memory Viewpoint' line of work, presented as a self-contained derivation
    • Why it matters
      • A usable mental model: if you can derive attention from memory requirements, you can reason about variants instead of memorizing architecture diagrams
      • It is an explanatory essay with heavy math notation, not a new result — the derivation is the product
    Source: alexwang.ai · Discuss: HN Discuss · 360 pts / 83 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe argument in-thread is about presentation, not correctness. The top critique: the essay uses dense math notation with no concrete worked example or pseudocode, which shuts out much of HN's software audience. Defenders note math posts regularly find a substantive audience here and that different explanations serve different readers; the original critic, downvoted, protests that 'if you don't understand something, shut up' is the wrong norm. Readers who followed the math treat the capacity progression as the payoff.

    Apple ML Research: Muse Spark Open with MLX on Mac Studio — 36+ tok/s on four machines

    Apple's ML Research post shows the 816B-parameter Muse Spark Open (42B active, evaluated in NVFP4) running locally with the new MLX release, on hardware from a single Mac Studio with up to 512 GB unified memory up to clusters linked by RDMA over Thunderbolt. Headline number: on a cluster of four M5 Ultra Mac Studios, Muse Spark Open generates over 36 tokens per second at an 8,192-token context — above average human reading speed, in Apple's framing. The post benchmarks pipeline parallelism over two machines and tensor parallelism over two and four, and also runs Mistral Medium 3.5 (128B) and Qwen 3.8 (27B) on M5 Ultra and the previous M3 Ultra, reporting time to first token, throughput and peak power, all measured with mlx_lm.benchmark.

    • The setup
      • M5's GPU Neural Accelerators add dedicated matrix multiplication; MoE sparsity is why an 816B model fits the memory-and-bandwidth budget at all
      • This is the technical companion to yesterday's Muse Spark Open launch — same model, measured on Apple's own stack
    • Why it matters
      • A frontier-class open model running privately on desk hardware, with published throughput and power numbers, is a concrete local-inference data point
      • The desk in question is four high-end Mac Studios; Apple has not announced 512 GB M5 pricing, and the 256 GB machine starts at $9,499 per in-thread pricing
    Source: machinelearning.apple.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 335 pts / 168 comments

    • Why it matters
