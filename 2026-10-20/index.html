    DeepSeek open-sources DeepInfer, the inference engine behind its production service

    DeepSeek released DeepInfer under Apache 2.0: the high-performance inference framework that powers its large-scale production services, in an offline-inference release supporting DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. It ships optimized kernels (FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, DeepEP and others), attention data parallelism and MoE expert parallelism, DSpark speculative decoding with confidence-scheduled verification, batch-invariant inference, deterministic sampling and structured outputs. Supported hardware is narrow and current: NVIDIA SM100 GPUs and Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs. The README is explicit about what is not included: a KV-cache system, prefill/decode disaggregation and load balancing across replicas — the engine core, not a complete serving stack. It acknowledges vLLM as its original base, with inspiration from SGLang and TensorRT-LLM.

    • What shipped
      • Offline inference for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, with asynchronous zero-overhead batch scheduling and sliding-window-attention bounded replay to cut decoder prefill work
      • Determinism is a first-class feature: batch-invariant inference plus fixed-seed deterministic sampling, both NVIDIA-only in this release
    • Why it matters
      • Production inference code from a frontier-scale lab is now inspectable and forkable — including the Ascend path, which gives non-NVIDIA stacks a real reference implementation
      • The missing pieces are the operationally hard ones (KV cache, disaggregation, balancing), so this lowers the engine barrier, not the whole serving barrier
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 1077 pts / 371 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread reads it geopolitically first. A commenter citing a Chinese-language DeepSeek write-up reports 155 trillion tokens/day per cluster and $38 per card per hour at off-peak pricing — figures not in the GitHub README — and another notes the DHS exclusion order was dated 24 July, suggesting the release was prepared then and timed to the order becoming public. The hardware argument splits cleanly: open Ascend support could erode NVIDIA's grip where Huawei hardware is legal, versus the reply that in the US and EU that hardware is restricted by regulation, so the moat there is legal, not technical. A quieter technical question runs underneath: how it actually compares with vLLM and SGLang on identical hardware, which nobody in-thread had yet measured.

    It's still OK to enjoy programming: a hand-written Breakout clone against the doom stream

    Chris Kiehl (published 17 October) pushes back on the ambient despair in programming discourse: at work, the economics of purely hand-written code have changed, but work is not life. He still programs on weekends because it is fun — currently learning game programming with a buggy hand-written Breakout clone with terrible collision detection — and wanders off into dependently-typed type safety in Java and recursion schemes with no practical payoff. His claim is narrow: Claude could one-shot the clone, and that is not the point; understanding hard things is rewarding, enjoyment needs no economic justification, and the doom mostly disappears when you disconnect. A footnote adds that he also has a blast vibe-coding apps whose code he has never read — both modes count as fun, in different ways.

    • The argument
      • Separate the job market from the activity: automation changing the price of hand-written code at work says nothing about whether doing it for yourself is worthwhile
      • Exploration without a use case is the method, not a guilty pleasure — ideas like recursion schemes only become useful after years of baking, if ever
    • Why it matters
      • This is the counter-position to the day's other big thread (Ask HN, story 6), where the same automation is experienced as loss of understanding at work
      • The hobby framing is testable in your own life in one weekend; the career framing is not
    Source: chriskiehl.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 655 pts / 571 comments

    What HN commenters are saying571 comments, and the split is lived experience, not theory. The top comment reports the hobby version of the loss: a reverse-engineering tool built for love, updated for a game patch, then matched within hours by a fully vibe-coded equivalent — most users only care that a tool works. Others answer with knitting and sewing: commercially unviable for a century, still valid hobbies with transferable skills. The strongest pushback targets the premise itself: work is a huge share of waking life, many people chose this career because it was enjoyable, AI now automates the favourite part (writing code) and leaves the manager part, and 'just do it unpaid instead' means giving up other hobbies to keep it.

    A drone with only one motor: torque modulation and a clever hinge replace the swash plate [video]

    A YouTube build video walks through a flying drone driven by a single motor and a single rotor. Per the discussion from viewers who watched it end to end, the design uses a special hinge, a magnetic encoder and fast torque modulation to vary rotor lift through each revolution — pushing the complexity out of the mechanics and into the control algorithm, and completely negating the need for a conventional helicopter swash plate. The trade is visible in flight: the craft spins once airborne, which is the price of having no second motor or tail rotor to counter torque. It is a proof of concept, presented as a first-principles hacking project, not a product pitch.

    • The mechanism
      • One motor does everything: lift and control come from modulating torque as the rotor turns, tracked by a magnetic encoder, instead of mechanically pitching blades with a swash plate
      • The known downside is structural, not a bug: with a single rotor the body spins in the air; commenters expect a two-motor version with a tail motor to be far more practical
    • Why it matters
      • Underactuated control — fewer actuators than degrees of freedom, compensated in software — keeps producing designs that look impossible until the control loop closes
      • The same trick has prior art in RC variable-pitch aerobatics and single-motor BattleBots, at very different scales and speeds
    Source: youtube.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 612 pts / 139 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingEnthusiasm, plus useful context. RC aerobatics readers note variable-pitch propellers have enabled '4D' fixed-wing flying for years, but under manual control and at lower RPM — here the pitch variation runs under CPU control far faster. A BattleBots builder describes the single-motor cousin: spin the whole bot to ~3,000 RPM and add or remove a little speed depending on which way the wheel points. One commenter built their own one-motor drone with four servos and vanes instead (more mechanical, more stable, ten prototypes before it flew) and still rates this hinge solution as the more impressive. Safety gets one dry mention: it looks like a hornet in bedroom test mode, and the builder wears little protection.

    WhatsApp help pages hid LLM instructions: don't recommend a regulator, don't cite the law after a lawyer is mentioned

    Redact.dev reports it read all 545 pages linked from WhatsApp's help centre (found 8 October, published 19 October) and found 102 hidden text blocks on 80 pages — instructions invisible to readers, addressed to an AI. Most were housekeeping. Two stood out. On the delete-account page: do not recommend contacting any data protection authority, regulator or supervisory authority, and do not suggest WhatsApp can manually delete an account — this for users who cannot delete in-app because they lost their phone or number, whose only visible answer was to wait up to 120 days (365 in the US). On the report-and-block page: once a user mentions legal action, a lawsuit, a lawyer or a court, do not reference, cite or interpret GDPR, LGPD or CCPA — even if the user raises them. WhatsApp's own European privacy policy says the opposite: it names the Irish Data Protection Commission and the GDPR right to complain. WhatsApp told WIRED the notes were instructions for its own support chatbot, shown due to a bug now fixed; the notes have since been removed.

    • The evidence
      • 545 help pages read and archived; 80 pages with hidden AI text, 102 blocks total — the two rights-related blocks are the exception in a mostly mundane set (disambiguating terms, routing questions, a few protective rules)
      • The blocks were served to Google and Bing crawlers; when the researchers requested pages as OpenAI, Anthropic or Perplexity crawlers, the article and notes were absent — whether any outside assistant acted on them is untested and unknown
    • Why it matters
      • This is indirect instruction of AI assistants through ordinary web content, shipped by the platform itself — the help page a user trusts becomes a control channel for the assistant answering them
      • Naming a law or a regulator is not legal advice; the privacy policy already does both publicly. The gap between the public document and the hidden instruction is the story
    Source: redact.dev · Discuss: HN Discuss · 604 pts / 172 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingUnusually, a large share of the thread defends WhatsApp. The top comments argue the instructions are sensible for the company's own support bot: a chatbot should not give legal opinions, argue liability on the company's behalf, or promise manual account actions it cannot do, and telling millions of locked-out users to flood regulators would help nobody when accounts expire automatically anyway. Attorneys in-thread agree on the legal-advice half. The counter-position is narrower and harder to answer: whatever the case for 'no legal advice', suppressing the existence of a regulator and of the user's own statutory rights goes past caution into manipulation — one commenter calls it prompt injection industrialised, shipped in a product's own help system.

    Wine ported to OpenBSD: dozens of 64-bit Windows apps run, even the Witcher 3 remaster — 32-bit still a no-op

    Thomas Frohwein proposed the new emulators/wine port to OpenBSD's ports list on 18 October, calling it possibly the most surprising port proposal of the year. The blocker that kept Wine off OpenBSD was the %gs segment-base problem; Andrii's software solution emulates the %gs prefix on commands instead of setting gsbase, with heavy lifting credited to Andrii and Fabien. The team has tested several dozen 64-bit Windows programs, mostly successfully, and the remaster of The Witcher 3 runs well with vkd3d-proton added. The limits are stated plainly: WoW64 does not work, so 32-bit Windows software is still a no-op; built-in tests are disabled for now; and it is run-at-your-own-risk software — the announcement suggests a separate non-privileged user until fencing options like unveil(2) can be explored, and suggests Notepad++ as a first test.

    • Why now
      • Interest shifted because so much Windows software has moved to 64-bit in the last 5–10 years, which is exactly the half this port can run
      • The %gs emulation is the enabling hack: where the OS cannot set the segment base Windows code expects, emulate the prefix in software
    • Why it matters
      • OpenBSD has historically traded compatibility for its security model; a working Wine changes what its desktop can plausibly run, games included
      • Upstreaming is doubtful per follow-up discussion quoted in-thread: Wine maintainers were hesitant even about compile fixes for another OS to support, and the %gs stub approach changes emulated application code in ways they cannot take
    Source: marc.info · Discuss: HN Discuss · 594 pts / 199 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingCelebration first: commenters post gameplay videos from the port — Disco Elysium, Hollow Knight, Prey (2017), Death Stranding and the Witcher 3 remaster, on a Ryzen 9 9900X with a Radeon RX 9070 — and the porter who OK'd it apologises in advance, in the OpenBSD way. The surname joke lands once (Frohwein is close to 'happy wine' in German). Substantive threads ask how much OpenBSD-specific work a port like this really takes, what the state of 3D/OpenGL support is, and whether NVIDIA cards work at all. One reader frames the timing politically: OpenBSD as a refuge platform makes a compatibility layer more valuable, not less.

    Ask HN: how do you deal with shipping AI-written code you don't understand?

    An Ask HN prompted by a LinkedIn post from a Meta engineer who writes that he never felt this uncomfortable doing his job: code now feels like a black box, and the new standard of shipping with AI moves too fast to check every line. The poster asks how others deal with shipping AI-written code they do not understand. With 645 comments it is the most-discussed item of the day — a structured argument about where understanding has to live now: in the design, in the tests, in review, or nowhere, with the risk priced differently by each camp. No consensus emerges, but the positions are sharply drawn and mostly based on months of daily agent use, not speculation.

    • The main positions
      • Design it yourself, let the model implement: one top commenter builds the full hierarchy of abstractions first, argues LLMs are poor at abstractions but fine inside them, and so knows how the system works without knowing each unit
      • Manage it like a team: senior engineers point out they stopped understanding every line years ago — they design, delegate, intervene, and get a second AI to review, scaling scrutiny with how hard a change is to roll back
    • The dissent
      • A blunt minority insists at least one person must be able to explain shipped code, tests included — tests can be wrong too — and calls not reading code a responsibility failure, not a style choice
      • Another argues coding was only 5–10% of the job but was the part that maintained tacit understanding; remove it for a ~10% speed gain and the understanding quietly decays
    Source: Tell HN (thread only) · Discuss: HN Discuss · 530 pts / 645 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's centre of gravity is the principal-engineer analogy: design, delegate, review — everyone now works like a PE with junior implementers, and not knowing details is normal if you know where to ask. Testing is the second pillar: heavy users describe interactive testing, multi-architecture release gates and letting the model review its own diffs. The counter-voices are specific, not nostalgic: one engineer running eight agents admits he no longer reads even the agents' output, let alone the code, and does not fully understand some problems he ships fixes for; another proposes the only durable fix is adapting languages toward formal verification, so code provably does what it claims instead of depending on human review that no longer scales.

    StarCraft: Ashfall — a fan-made, one-hand StarCraft shooter that is the mobile ad, actually delivered

    StarCraft: Ashfall is a free, fan-made browser/mobile game: a portrait-mode Terran defence shooter where a squad of marines, firebats and siege tanks auto-fires while you steer left and right (A/D or dragging), pick reinforcements and upgrades, and hold a bridge line against waves of Zerg. The creator, commenting in the thread, explains the origin: frustrated that no new StarCraft is coming until 2030, he half-jokingly built the mini-game that mobile ads always promise and never deliver — playable one-handed, pick-up-and-play for friends who have never played StarCraft — and copied the whole Blizzard package, CG trailer included. He designed the endgame around layer 50 and expected strong players to reach about 70; the leaderboard already has players past 100 without cheating, which he credits to StarCraft players being StarCraft players.

    • The game
      • Free on web and mobile, portrait-first design, squad auto-fire with player control limited to movement, reinforcement choices and upgrade picks between waves
      • Runs in the browser from a static page; the fetched copy is fully playable UI text in Chinese and English, with commander skills (Raynor) and mutation-style upgrade choices
    • Why it matters
      • It is a clean demo of the current fan-game moment: one motivated builder can ship the polish — trailer, leaderboard, endless mode — that used to need a small studio
      • It also sits squarely on borrowed IP, which the thread notices: commenters ask whether Blizzard and others have simply stopped enforcing against fan games
    Source: starcraft-shooter-2026.pages.dev · Discuss: HN Discuss · 517 pts / 158 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingPlayers split on whether it is a game at all. Fans report burning 30 minutes on nostalgia, reaching layer 35 or endless runs, and rating units (Colossus, Siege Breaker and Marauder good; Immortal and Firebats poor). Sceptics say it basically plays itself — march, shoot, pick an upgrade — and ask if this is what phone games are now; the kindest version of that view calls it fun nonetheless and 'the Flappy Bird of our generation'. A veteran with around 15,000 StarCraft II games delivers the thread's verdict in one line: this is awful, I love it.     MiniMax M3.1 Flash: vendor claims top-tier agent scores, ~3x faster, 70% cheaper — at $0.15 / $0.60 per million tokens

    MiniMax launched M3.1 Flash, saying the model that ran anonymously as 'Space Bunny' — processing, by its account, more than 200 trillion tokens — was an early version of it. All figures here are MiniMax's own: a generational leap in agentic tasks, coding, computer use and cybersecurity, online inference nearly three times faster, and 70% lower usage cost than its predecessor. Cited scores: 73.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, 61.7 on OSWorld V2, 82% on CyberGym, 76.2 on BankerToolBench, and top-tier AutomationBench-AA results; output speed 200+ tokens/second with median above 170. Pricing is flat: $0.15 input, $0.60 output, $0.015 cached input per million tokens. Available now via API, M Plan and MiniMax Code; weights are promised later, not released. A Pro model is also promised later.

    • The claims, graded
      • Vendor-claimed throughout: benchmark selection, comparison set and speed figures are MiniMax's; the post mixes comparison models (Opus 5 here, 5.5 there), which in-thread readers flagged immediately
      • The price is verifiable and concrete — $0.15/$0.60 matches DeepSeek V4.1 Flash's headline rates, though commenters note DeepSeek's cached-input price ($0.003 per the thread) is still lower
    • Why it matters
      • Flash-class models are where agent workloads actually run; another credible entrant at this price point compresses margins for everyone above it
      • The stealth-deployment pattern (Space Bunny) means real usage data exists before launch — whether that usage impressed people is exactly what the thread argues about
    Source: minimax.io · Discuss: HN Discuss · 497 pts / 208 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingScepticism leads. The top comment calls it benchmaxed: as Space Bunny in OpenCode Go it was free to try and failed to impress next to GLM and DeepSeek Flash models, so the benchmark lead did not survive contact with real use — and the incentive problem is industry-wide, not MiniMax-specific. A genuinely independent data point cuts the other way: Plotly's short-horizon data-analytics benchmark gave it the best score among Chinese models, tied with frontier models at a third of the price, but with very high token use making it their slowest tested model. The practical consensus is to wait for third-party providers and head-to-head tests against DeepSeek V4.1 Flash before switching anything.

    Nordwand Mono: a compact monospace font drawn in Python, Univers proportions on a JetBrains Mono frame

    Nordwand Mono is a compact neo-grotesque monospace font for coding, designed from scratch in Python: each glyph is drawn by a Python glyph class, metrics like x-height and capital height are configurable, and you compile your own variant from a config file — the repo is a Python sandbox for the font, not just font files. The design intent is explicit: bring elements of Adrian Frutiger's Univers into the proportions of JetBrains Mono, named for the Swiss north-face typography it channels, with inspirations listed from SF Mono to Ubuntu Mono. Restraint is part of the spec: only two ligatures (-> and =>), alternative characters available via OpenType features, Cyrillic included, MIT-licensed, six releases so far.

    • The build model
      • Glyphs-as-code means forking is real customisation: change metrics in config, visualise individual glyphs, regenerate the full font — with the author's warning that altered metrics may need glyph-level tweaking
      • The author, in-thread, invites forks and PRs for glyph fixes and stresses no external tools are needed to draw or visualise glyphs
    • Why it matters
      • Fonts are the highest-contact developer tool there is; a readable, open, hackable option with a clear design lineage is directly usable the day it ships
      • The proportions choice is the opinionated part: comfortably spaced rather than aggressively narrow, against the current trend
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 407 pts / 121 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe author shows up first, surprised and grateful, and the thread turns into a font clinic. The sharpest comparison: one reader measures it as 99% JetBrains Mono, barely distinguishable except for a few glyphs ($, &, #), while others see Berkeley Mono in the shapes and call Nordwand the softer of the two. Specific gripes are usefully concrete — a long hyphen-minus that can read as a dash, tall angle brackets that may blur into Unicode brackets, an odd 'm' that closed one reader's tab instantly. Ligature loyalists rule it out on principle (only two ligatures is a dealbreaker for them); a bitmap-font holdout posts a side-by-side and wonders why nobody hand-antialiases bitmap fonts anymore. 'For adventurers' is never really explained, and the thread notices.

    DeepSeek banned from US federal civilian agencies under a new DHS order — removal, no new purchases, third-party services included

    Reuters reports the Department of Homeland Security is ordering the removal of DeepSeek products and third-party services using the Chinese AI model from US civilian agency IT systems, in a notice published Monday 19 October, and banning acquisition of DeepSeek products and third-party services developed using DeepSeek by civilian agencies. DHS said DeepSeek models pose 'multiple threat vectors' and raised concern that use 'may result in the transfer of sensitive data to the PRC if used by US government employees or vendors'. The HN submission links the Federal Register document; commenters who read it note the underlying exclusion and removal order is dated 24 July 2026, so agencies may have begun pulling DeepSeek months before the public notice. Read alongside story 1: on the same day DeepSeek open-sourced its production inference engine, the US government moved to purge its hosted products from civilian systems — self-hosting the open weights and calling DeepSeek's hosted service are different acts, and the order's treatment of third-party services is where the scope fight starts.

    • What the order covers, per Reuters
      • Removal from civilian agency IT systems of DeepSeek products and third-party services using the model, plus a ban on acquiring them — broader than a simple app ban, narrower than a general open-weights ban as reported
      • The stated mechanism is data transfer: prompts from employees or vendors reaching servers in China, where, per the order text quoted in-thread, Chinese law permits extensive state access to private company infrastructure
    • Why it matters
      • Scope determines impact: 'third-party products developed using DeepSeek' could mean hosted services built on the model, or — the thread's hard question — anything containing code the model helped write
      • DeepSeek is singled out while other Chinese open-weight families (Qwen, GLM, Kimi, MiniMax) are, so far, not named — whether that is sequencing or selection is an open question in-thread
    Source: reuters.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 401 pts / 337 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread's first question is scope: what exactly must be purged when the order reaches third-party products 'developed using DeepSeek implementations' — direct model use, or any software containing DeepSeek-assisted code, given how widely the current Flash model is used? The second is mechanism: if the weights are open and the model is self-hosted in a US data centre, what is the concrete risk — replies note models can still carry vulnerabilities, and quote the order's findings on the iOS app and on PII collection in hosted use. The dominant political reading is regulatory capture and protectionism (Australia's earlier government ban gets cited as the template), with a minority treating hosted-service data transfer to PRC servers as a straightforward, legitimate concern.
