    Critical PHP release today: two RCE flaws rated CVSS 9.2 — patch at 14:00 UTC, details already out

    The PHP security team pre-announced a critical release for Wednesday 21 October 2026 at 14:00 UTC covering two vulnerabilities, both rated CVSS v4.0 9.2: CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619. The announcement gave no technical details and warned the release could move earlier if details leaked or active exploitation appeared. In the event, per commenters linking php.net and the GitHub advisories, fixed versions were released slightly early with full details: remote code execution via out-of-memory corruption, and RCE via stale global state. The practical instruction is unchanged: be ready to update the moment packages land, because a public patch plus public details is also an exploit-writing kit. A commenter reports Dutch DigiD (government login), broadcaster RTL and parliament sites shut down pre-emptively — commenter-reported, not independently verified here.

    • What is known
      • Two critical RCE-class flaws, both CVSS 9.2, affecting PHP broadly; scheduled coordinated release 21 Oct 14:00 UTC, with fixed versions and advisories appearing slightly early per in-thread links
      • No exploitation evidence was cited in the original pre-announcement; the early-release trigger was detail leakage, which the patch publication itself creates
    • Why it matters
      • PHP still runs a large share of the public web, including government login infrastructure — the blast radius is measured in hundreds of thousands of instances that must patch in the same window
      • The shutdown-vs-stay-up choice is the real decision: shutting down avoids exposure but guarantees downtime; staying up bets you can patch before exploits mature
    Source: news-web.php.net · Discuss: HN Discuss · 1334 pts / 553 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread splits on disclosure timing. One camp wanted the patch earlier; the counter-argument is that publishing a fix makes exploit construction easier, so a single coordinated moment plus loud advance warning — giving operators the option to shut down first — minimises total damage. Operators ask the unglamorous question: how do you tell if you were already hit (new files, unexpected POSTs in access logs)? A longer critique argues the deeper problem is 1990s PHP app architecture — one shared database role, writable uploads, no row-level security — so a single RCE still hands over the whole database.

    eMudhra intermediate CA keys were single bytes — anyone could impersonate any site since April 2025

    The private keys of eight eMudhra intermediate CA certificates — six TLS issuers (DV, OV, EV, two generations) and two code-signing CAs, created April 2025 and cross-signed by Sectigo — were incorrectly generated and are recoverable from the public certificates themselves. Reporter Wayne demonstrated compromise by signing the message 'We have a problem here - Wayne' with each key and reported it to the root programs on 17 October. The intermediates chain to roots trusted by Mozilla, Microsoft, Apple and Chrome. Browsers blocked them fast (Firefox and Chrome 17 Oct, Apple 19 Oct, Microsoft 20 Oct) and the CA revoked them, but clients that do not consume revocation — most non-browser TLS stacks — stay exposed until their trust store updates. No CT-log entries exist for these intermediates, so CT-enforcing browsers could not have been attacked via forged certs; non-CT clients are the open question.

    • The flaw
      • Keys were incorrectly generated at creation in April 2025 — per eMudhra's Mozilla incident account quoted in-thread, a flaw in the key-generation ceremony script plus a wrap/unwrap test deviation produced a second, weak key pair that was used by mistake
      • An attacker with any one intermediate key could issue a trusted certificate for any website or service
    • Why it matters
      • Browser remediation took hours; ecosystem remediation takes months — the disclosure page lists patched versions across OS ca-certificates packages, Go, Node, Python certifi, Rust, Electron and dozens more, each a separate update path
      • macOS is the sharp example: the OS blocks the intermediates, but the built-in curl/git/python3 verify against /etc/ssl/cert.pem without revocation and that file had not been updated
    Source: emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 1090 pts / 424 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingShock first: the top comment notes one private key is literally the number 4 — 'compromised' in scare quotes, because nothing was stolen. A Mozilla voice stresses how uneven the impact is: OneCRL remediated Firefox in hours and CRLite scales revocation for browsers, while the rest of the ecosystem has made almost no revocation investment. Others flag the second-order problem — countless binaries vendor their own CA store, so patching the system bundle does not patch them — and share one-liners to distrust eMudhra on macOS, Ubuntu and Windows.

    Show HN: Permit Pending — SimCity where every building needs a hearing, an appeal and re-election

    Permit Pending (Peter Cai) is a free, browser-based city builder with one twist on the SimCity formula: you are not a god, you are an elected mayor. Every apartment block, bus lane and factory goes through zoning hearings, neighbour objections, council votes, lawsuits and appeals, over a real underlying economy where residents have ages, incomes and homes, and businesses hire, grow and leave. The landing page's worked example is the whole game in miniature: rezone a lot for 12 stories in month 3, watch homeowners organise over parking and 'neighbourhood character', trade height for parking until the project no longer pays, get appealed anyway — and face election in month 48 with rents still rising and both sides blaming you. No account or download; saves are local and it works offline after the first visit. Sandbox forever, no win screen — the challenge is staying in office long enough to build anything.

    • The simulation
      • Opposition is systemic, not scripted flavour: new housing lowers rents and angers homeowners, industry brings jobs and smog, transit attracts development and development attracts opponents
      • Re-election every four years, with high-turnout groups wielding outsized power; heatmaps and a local paper, the Gazette, explain which decision made which voters angry
    • Why it matters
      • It models the actual binding constraint on real cities — process and politics, not engineering or money — which is why a 12-story building becomes a 6-story building that no longer pencils out
      • As a Show HN it is also a craft signal: a complete, free, offline-capable game with a point of view, shipped by one builder

    iPadOS 27 broke presenter mode: tap another app and your private notes hit the classroom screen

    Teddy Svoronos, who teaches from an iPad projected via AirPlay, reports a regression in iPadOS 27: previously the external display stayed on the presenting app (GoodNotes handouts, Keynote, PowerPoint) while he used the iPad itself for notes, student call lists and hidden poll results. As of iPadOS 27, with multiple windows open while AirPlaying — Stage Manager on or off — tapping anything other than the presenting app snaps the external display to the whole iPad screen, exposing exactly the material students must not see. Recovery differs by app: GoodNotes returns to presenter view when tapped back, Keynote exits the presentation entirely, PowerPoint lands in a limbo requiring a restart. His proposed rule is simple: an app in presenter mode keeps precedence on the external display until the presentation stops, the document closes or the app quits. He filed feedback FB23615646 and says the behaviour persists in current betas.

    • The mechanism
      • In iPadOS 26, which app controlled the external display was obscure (swipe up, tap the dock app); Apple's fix makes the most recently tapped app take over — simpler to explain, worse in use
      • This is not an edge case for presenters: private speaker notes, poll results and call lists are the standard second-screen workflow in teaching and conferences
    • Why it matters
      • A confidentiality failure, not a cosmetic bug: one tap publishes private notes to a room, live
      • Until Apple changes it, his recommendation stands: do not rely on iPadOS 27 for teaching or presenting with anything private on the device

    The future of CSS, Interop 2027 edition: functions, squircles, border-shape and inherit()

    Josh Comeau previews the CSS features he voted for in Mozilla's Interop 2027 ranking — the cross-vendor programme (Google, Apple, Microsoft, Mozilla, Igalia, Bocoup) whose focus areas reliably reach near-100% browser support within about a year, as Subgrid did in 2023 (12% to 91% of tests passing). His top picks: CSS functions (@function with result:, already in Chromium — his example converts polar to cartesian coordinates for animation); corner-shape (squircle, bevel, notch, scoop — unlike clip-path it changes the real border box, so borders and shadows follow); border-shape (draw the border itself with lines, arcs and Béziers, animatable); anchored container queries (finally learn in CSS whether an anchored tooltip flipped to its fallback position); inherit() as a function (calc(inherit() - 4px) for correct nested radii — unimplemented anywhere, and he doubts it makes 2027); plus scroll-triggered animations, reading-flow/reading-order for correct tab order under responsive layouts, and more.

    • Usable now vs later
      • Already in Chrome/Edge and progressive-enhancement-safe today: corner-shape and border-shape degrade gracefully in Firefox/Safari; CSS functions are Chromium-only for now
      • Not implemented anywhere: inherit() — included as a wish, with the author himself sceptical it survives the Interop cut
    • Why it matters
      • Interop is the closest thing the web platform has to a shipping schedule: a feature on the list is a feature you can plan on, which is why a public ranking vote matters
      • The direction is consistent — CSS absorbing layout and interaction work (tooltips, scroll animations, tab order) that currently needs custom JavaScript
    Source: joshwcomeau.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 483 pts / 200 comments

    What shall I teach my son? Education's purpose when programming stops paying

    Steve Krouse (Val Town), expecting his first child, asks what education is for when his economically valuable skill — programming — suddenly feels less valuable in the age of AGI. The essay (19 October) runs through Neil Postman's The End of Education (1995): schools have survived the radio, motion pictures, MOOCs and every Edison-style prediction that technology would replace teachers (Edison, 1913: books obsolete within ten years), because their stated purpose and their real perpetuating forces — endowments, prestige, childcare — are different things. Krouse's own one-word purpose, 'resourceful', now troubles him: an AI agent with a tool loop is resourcefulness perfected, the way the Dewey Decimal System replaced memorisation. His landing is Postman's method rather than any single answer: purposes can be invented and argued for (Spaceship Earth, the American Experiment, the World Makers), the medium is the message, and children should be invited to help craft the purpose of their own education.

    • The argument
      • Lindy is the load-bearing idea: institutions that survived God, radio and the internet should be bet to survive AGI in roughly current form — and technologists doubting schools is itself a Lindy tradition
      • The wobbling leg is 'earn a living': the market was his objective arbiter of educational good (Jobs, Gates, Einstein as escapees), and AI mathematics is now firing on that arbiter too
    • Why it matters
      • This is the personal version of the day's labour question — not 'will AI take jobs' in aggregate, but what a specific parent teaches a specific child when his own skill devalues
      • The actionable residue is small and real: how you teach (doing with, not doing to) outlasts what you teach

    Eight bits of trust: eMudhra's CA private keys are 4, 56 and 115 — cracked in milliseconds, trusted until 2039

    Scott Helme independently brute-forced all eight compromised eMudhra intermediate keys (story 2) from the public certificates Mozilla attached to its announcement — on his laptop, in under a second. The P-384 private keys are single bytes: EM OV TLS CA G3C is d=4, EM EV TLS CA G3C is 56, G3B is 115; the full table runs 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230, 237 across the six TLS and two code-signing intermediates. A valid P-384 key should be a random 384-bit number; these are 47 zero bytes plus one byte — at most 255 trials of d×G. Nothing about the certificates looks structurally wrong, which is the point: the only way to know is to try. His containment argument is the hard half: nobody stole anything, the keys were effectively public since April 2025 (a 17-month window, unknowable exploitation), and while CT enforcement protects browsers, non-browser clients (curl, Python, Node, Go, package managers, CI, IoT) mostly do not check CT or revocation — he demonstrates a forged api.stripe.com certificate verifying cleanly on his own machines. Unpatchable devices trust these chains until the roots expire in July 2039. eMudhra's later account, quoted in his update: a ceremony-script bug created a second weak key pair on the HSM and a human error used the wrong keys for the CSR.

    • The numbers
      • Eight keys, eight bits each: 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230, 237 — recovered by trying d=1, 2, 3… against the public key, at most 255 steps per key
      • Exposure window: certificates created April 2025, reported 17 October 2026 — 17 months during which anyone who looked could have done the same
    • Why it matters
      • The fix list is the ecosystem: update every CA bundle (OS, certifi, Node, Homebrew), rebuild container images — and accept that frozen-firmware devices cannot be fixed at all
      • Code signing is the quiet second front: Windows trusts the eMudhra CS root directly, so malware signed with these keys looks publisher-signed where revocation is unchecked
    Source: scotthelme.co.uk · Discuss: HN Discuss · 426 pts / 188 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread mixes horror with opportunism: if unpatchable IoT will trust forged certs until 2039, can the same keys be used to jailbreak those devices with owner-serving certificates — one commenter links claims it is already happening. The original reporter, Wayne, adds how it was found: Google's paranoid_crypto library, run over the CCADB corpus as a quick pre-check — 'most of my time was spent going over the basics everyone assumed someone else had already done', with a fuller sweep finding no further hits. The dry summary with the most support: in retrospect, having 100 trusted certificate authorities might have been excessive.

    Google open-sources TorchTPU: a native PyTorch backend for TPUs, not another XLA workaround

    Google published TorchTPU (google-pytorch/torch_tpu, Apache 2.0): a native PyTorch backend for Tensor Processing Units, providing custom ATen kernel implementations for TPU, a compilation cache for XLA computations, distributed training support (data and tensor parallelism), torch.compile() graph-mode integration, and examples across Llama, Qwen, ResNet, DLRM, minGPT and UNet. Install is pip (torch_tpu 0.2.0, Python 3.11+, with the CPU torch wheel index) or a Bazel source build — the README is unusually honest about build traps, including why --config=wheel_common is mandatory (without it every per-version module ships its own backend copy and import aborts on duplicate registrations) and how to strip Remote Build Execution if you lack credentials. Notebooks are Marimo files, and the practical on-ramp is a TPU VM — a v6e GCE instance is suggested as easiest. This replaces the old fight-XLA-to-make-PyTorch-run story with first-party support.

    • What shipped
      • ATen kernels, XLA compilation cache, distributed training and inference examples, debugging/benchmarking utilities — a backend, not a translation shim
      • Version 0.2.0 on PyPI; building from source is Bazel-only and documented in depth, RBE caveats included
    • Why it matters
      • PyTorch is where the models and the hiring pool already are; a credible native TPU path makes TPUs a real alternative back end instead of a JAX-only island
      • Amazon is doing the analogous work for Trainium (torch-neuronx, noted in-thread) — every accelerator vendor now needs a PyTorch front door
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 365 pts / 77 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingA short, technical thread.     Muse's share links were publicly findable — one passive-DNS query listed 6,800, and the pages behind them held real secrets

    Ars Technica reports Claroc's 'GhostShares' findings against Muse, Meta's standalone personal-assistant app. Shared conversations, searches and images got pages on cf.metaaiusercontent.com with the conversation's title in the subdomain — and subdomains are exactly what passive DNS databases record when a recipient opens a link. Claroc's Asaf Aprozper says a single passive-DNS query enumerated 6,800 share links; opening a few hundred turned up material plainly not meant to be public: confidential executive-search call notes naming a frontier AI lab's internal contact and meeting time, a national marketing company's internal revenue review marked for its CEO, a small-business loan application with the amount requested, a to-do list carrying a bank fraud alert and a partial account number, a real-estate investor's performance report with property addresses and exact mortgage figures, and one workplace's full-year staff vacation calendar. Anyone holding a link could open the page behind it — finding the links was the only barrier, and it was no barrier. Claroc reported through Meta's bug bounty on 8 October and tried LinkedIn as well, and got no reply either way; Meta told Ars it has paused the sharing feature while it reviews the report and considers changes to how the links can be discovered. No fix is claimed, and Ars reports no evidence that anyone beyond the researchers exploited the discoverability.

    • The flaw, precisely
      • Conversation titles sat in share-link subdomains on cf.metaaiusercontent.com; passive DNS records subdomains when recipients click, so one query listed 6,800 links — discovery, not hacking
      • The pages behind the links opened for anyone holding the URL, and a few hundred opened links held genuine personal and business secrets — that sampled contents exposure is the harm, not any file count
    • Why it matters
      • Capability URLs fail when anything identifying rides in observable channels — DNS lookups are recorded and searchable, so titles never belong in subdomains
      • The response timeline matters: reported 8 October, no reply to the researchers, sharing paused only after Ars asked — and the fix itself is still under review, not shipped
    Source: arstechnica.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 258 pts / 112 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingSarcasm first ('Meta, careless with user data? Unbelievable'), then a real design argument. One reader asks the clarifying question: did users intend these conversations for specific recipients behind an unguessable capability URL, or were conversations effectively public behind obscure URLs by default? The DNS point gets the technical vote: subdomain names land in passive-DNS databases, so secrets and identities do not belong there regardless of intent. A builder of a small European personal-assistant platform adds the sympathetic version — harness customisation complexity explodes with real users — before noting that infinite money should still buy a working bug-bounty reply.
