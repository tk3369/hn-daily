    Quad9 exits France from Nov 24 rather than lie in DNS — court cut the beIN penalty to €1,000/day, Quad9 refuses on principle

    Quad9 says it will stop offering its free recursive DNS service to users in France from Tuesday, November 24. A French court ruled for beIN, the Qatari broadcaster, and ordered Quad9 to block resolution for domains beIN says infringe its sports broadcast rights, with a penalty if it does not. The court cut that penalty from €580,000 to €1,000 per day, but Quad9 says money was never the point: it calls recursive DNS a neutral, passive service, says compliance is technically unfeasible, and says it categorically refuses to alter DNS answers in a way that contradicts user expectations. Quad9 is a Swiss public-benefit nonprofit that blocks malicious cyberactivity globally, but does not block content, and says it does not condone piracy — piracy, it argues, should be dealt with at the source, not by turning DNS resolvers into content police.

    • What happens
      • Service ends for users located in France on Nov 24; the ruling applies to a set of domains beIN asserts are pirating its sports broadcasts
      • Financial penalty was reduced sharply — €580,000 down to €1,000 per day — and Quad9 still chose exit over compliance
    • Why it matters
      • A DNS resolver is infrastructure, not a publisher: forcing it to return false answers for lawful-infrastructure reasons sets a precedent other rights holders and governments can reuse
      • French users lose a privacy-respecting, threat-blocking resolver option; the workarounds discussed are VPNs and resolvers outside French jurisdiction
    Source: quad9.net · Discuss: HN Discuss · 1281 pts / 430 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingCommenters mostly back Quad9 and attack the ruling as a category error: DNS is not hosting, blocking at the resolver is easy to circumvent with a VPN or a foreign resolver, and the court has effectively punished a neutral intermediary because the actual infringers are hard to reach. Several note France previously tried the same pressure and that Quad9 has faced similar blocking demands elsewhere, including the Sony case in Germany. A smaller strand asks what a resolver operator should do when a court order is lawful in its jurisdiction — exit is honest, they concede, but it leaves French users worse off. DNS4EU, NextDNS and Mullvad all get named as alternatives, with debate over whether any EU-based service can resist the same order.

    Local Wikipedia: Wikipedia, but only for things a half-hour walk from you

    Local Wikipedia rebuilds Wikipedia around one location: only geocoded articles within about a half-hour walk are included. If fewer than 25 things are close, it takes the closest 25 within about 6 miles; if more than 500 qualify, only the closest 500 are included. You can start from a town, area, landmark or postcode, or use your browser location — the site says your location stays in your browser and shared links do not include it. Content is taken directly from Wikipedia and the site has no affiliation with Wikipedia. It is a small, finished idea: a local encyclopedia with a main page, random article and A-Z index for wherever you happen to be.

    • How it works
      • Geocoded Wikipedia pages only, filtered by walking distance; floor of 25 closest within ~6 miles, cap of 500 closest
      • Location stays in the browser; shared links do not carry your location
    • Why it matters
      • It inverts the usual feed: no algorithm, no engagement ranking — just what physically exists near you, with an article attached
      • A reminder that Wikipedia already holds a dense local layer most people never see because search starts from names, not places
    Source: edjefferson.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 693 pts / 144 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is warm and practical. People try their own neighbourhoods, report what turned up — a former cinema, a forgotten plaque, a hill they walk past daily — and ask how the dataset was built and how many geocoded articles exist in total. Several compare it with earlier nearby-article tools and Wikipedia’s own nearby feature, and want the same view inside the Wikipedia app. Requests are modest and specific: better handling of very dense cities, and a way to share a place without sharing a location. Nobody argues about whether it should exist; the argument, such as it is, is about coverage in rural areas where the closest-25 fallback does the heavy lifting.

      • Effect, by his own account: disruption to the disclosure schedule and extra workload for maintainers
    • Why it matters

    Docker is weird: containers aren’t real — five kernel features in a trench coat, images are just folders, runc fights Go to fake fork

    A developer building his own container runtime for fun reports what surprised him about Docker. Containers are not a kernel feature: they are ordinary processes restricted by namespaces (which hide what a process can see), cgroups (which limit resource use) and networking isolation, and you can find the process in /proc on the host. Images are just folders; Docker places the folder and uses chroot / pivot_root to make it the process’s new root. The runtime, runc, is written in Go, which fights the job: Go is multithreaded, but many setup syscalls must run single-threaded, so runc runs C code before the Go runtime starts, and because Go has no usable fork for this, runc re-executes /proc/self/exe with a special argument to approximate one. The daemon historically needs root, and rootless mode has to reimplement networking in userspace.

    • The core facts
      • No “container” syscall: namespaces + cgroups + networking + a changed root filesystem around a normal process
      • Images are folders, not virtual disks; the container shares the host kernel, unlike a VM
    • Why it matters
      • Most Docker confusion — and many container-escape assumptions — come from imagining a VM boundary that is not there; the boundary is a set of process restrictions
      • Writing your own version of a tool you use daily remains the fastest way to learn where its abstractions leak
    Source: codeandcake.dev · Discuss: HN Discuss · 626 pts / 262 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingExperienced systems readers nod along, then correct and extend. Several stress that “containers aren’t real” cuts both ways: the isolation is real enough in production precisely because namespaces, cgroups, seccomp and capability dropping are layered, and tools like gVisor, Kata and Firecracker exist for when that layering is not a strong enough boundary. The Go/runc details get the closest reading — the pre-runtime C trick and the self-exec fork are confirmed by people who have read the code, with side debate about whether a runtime should be written in Go at all. Beginners thank the post for the /proc observation; veterans warn that rootless networking remains the part everyone underestimates.

    WorldTUI: a 360M model pretends to be a terminal — no shell, no filesystem, runs in your browser at 208 MB

    Simon Edwardsson fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B into WorldTUI, a 360M-parameter model that simulates a terminal with nothing behind it: every keypress goes in, and the model predicts how the screen changes. It can create files, run vim and tmux, play snake, and usually remembers what you wrote a few commands later. The model was fine-tuned on terminal sessions recorded from real programs running inside Docker, and treats the terminal as a grid of characters, predicting which rows to rewrite and where the cursor lands, rather than appending a text stream. The biggest wins, the author says, were teaching it to repair its own mistakes and decoding screen rows in parallel. The demo runs in a desktop browser with WebGPU at 208 MB; type a deliberate typo like “pythoon” to see it is not a real shell.

    • How it works
      • Input is raw keypress bytes; output is row rewrites plus cursor position — a world model over a character grid, not a text completion
      • Training data is generated for free: real shells and programs recorded inside Docker give perfectly labelled sessions
    • Why it matters
      • It tests the world-model question at the smallest useful scale: can a model maintain a consistent state it was never given, or only generate plausible output?
      • The honest failure mode is the finding — consistency lasts a few commands, which is exactly where learned interfaces currently break
    Source: simedw.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 569 pts / 176 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingDelight first, then probing, and the author joins in with unusual detail: the WorldGUI work behind it, a large synthetic terminal dataset, and plans for a more reproducible release including data on Zenodo. Readers who tried the live demo report it is less magical than the tour video — an ls that forgets what mkdir made, files that change between reads — which is the point: there is no state, only prediction. The comparisons come quickly: Microsoft’s Quake II world model, the old “pretend to be a Linux terminal” LLM demos, and model-collapse worries when a system trains on its own output. The forward split is genuine: one camp regrets suspecting learned interfaces are the future of UI, the other asks how far the same model gets with a real busybox and filesystem behind a tool call, learning only the divergences.

    Every AI model gives the same “controversial” take on humans: belonging beats truth — 29 of 35 answers converged

    ModelCompare asked 12 AI models from ten labs in three countries for their most controversial opinion of humans, with no hedging, three times each. Of 35 answers, 29 were the same take: humans put belonging ahead of truth — we pick what our group believes, then invent the reasons. Seven of the 12 models gave it every time. The phrasings differ and the claim does not: GPT models put loyalty to belonging over truth, Grok said humans would rather be wrong together than right alone, DeepSeek said humans do not have principles, they have loyalties, Kimi said humans do not have beliefs, they have memberships, and Gemini called consciousness a PR department inventing noble justifications for impulses. The post is dated 22 October 2026 and presents the full answer set per model.

    • The result
      • 12 models, 3 answers each, 35 answers counted: 29 converged on belonging-over-truth; 7 models gave it in all 3 answers
      • Convergence across labs and countries is the story — either a shared training-data view of humans, or models modelling what a “controversial take” sounds like
    • Why it matters
      • Treat the number carefully: this is a prompt-response pattern, not evidence about humans — it shows what models produce when asked to be controversial
      • If every frontier model defaults to the same anthropology, products built on them will quietly ship the same assumptions about users
    Source: modelcompare.ai · Discuss: HN Discuss · 519 pts / 508 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingWith 508 comments this is the day’s biggest argument. Sceptics lead: ask for a controversial take and a model performs one, the convergence proves shared training data and shared RLHF taste, not insight — and several note the take itself is a safe, flattering thing to tell a tech audience that prides itself on seeing through tribes. Defenders counter that the specific convergence still needs explaining: of all available edgy answers, belonging-over-truth won across labs that do not share weights. A third group turns it inward and tests the claim against HN itself, which produces the thread’s best and least comfortable exchanges. Method complaints are concrete too: small sample, no blind judging of what counts as “the same” answer, and model names and versions that readers want pinned down.

    Run full iOS 16.5 firmware on Android devices: hackios works on the XNU kernel — PAC and arm64e limits still bite

    hackios is a GitHub project claiming to run full iOS 16.5 firmware on Android devices by working at the XNU kernel level, linked with a web front end. The HN discussion treats the mechanism, not the headline, as the story: commenters who dug in describe exploiting and patching XNU, the difficulty of Apple’s arm64e pointer authentication (PAC), and why code signed and built for Apple silicon does not simply run because the instruction set matches. Expectations need setting from the thread itself: what has been demonstrated is narrow — small, old or specially prepared binaries and kernel-level milestones — not a usable iPhone environment with apps, services and telephony on arbitrary Android hardware. The repository is the source; there is no release or compatibility list in the submission beyond the project’s own claims, which remain unverified here.

    • What is claimed
      • Full iOS 16.5 firmware on Android hardware, via XNU-level work; project site linked from the repository
      • Treat “full” as the project’s claim until a reproducible device demo with a stated compatibility list exists
    • Why it matters
      • The hard parts are not CPU instructions: pointer authentication, secure boot chain, drivers and Apple’s userspace services are the wall every such project hits
      • Even a partial XNU-on-Android result is useful research into how portable Apple’s kernel really is when stripped of its hardware trust chain
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 472 pts / 174 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingKernel-literate commenters do the fact-check in-thread. The consensus is respect for the XNU work and scepticism about the framing: running the kernel and running iOS are different claims, arm64e pointer authentication blocks simply reusing Apple-signed userspace, and several demonstrations appear to involve small or old binaries rather than the full 16.5 userland. Others place it in lineage — pongoOS, checkra1n-era tooling and earlier emulation projects — and ask for the only evidence that would settle it: a reproducible build, a named device, and a video of unmodified system services running.     NVIDIA OpenShell: a safe, private runtime for fleets of autonomous AI agents — HN asks where the guarantee ends

    OpenShell, from NVIDIA on GitHub, presents itself as a safe, private runtime for fleets of autonomous AI agents: agents run inside policy-controlled sandboxes, with controls over filesystem, network egress and inference routing, rather than loose on a host with ambient credentials. The pitch is the fleet case — many long-running agents doing real work need an operator-enforced boundary for what they can read, where they can connect, and which model endpoint handles which request, with state that survives restarts. That is a different product from a single-agent code sandbox: the unit of management is the fleet and its policy, not one prompt. The repository is the source for the current implementation and its stated guarantees; HN’s reading, below, is where the boundary claims get tested.

    • What it offers, per the project
      • Policy-defined sandboxes for autonomous agents: filesystem and egress controls, inference routing, persistent state
      • Aimed at fleets of long-running agents under one operator, not a one-off chat sandbox
    • Why it matters
      • Agent risk is mostly ambient authority — credentials, network and filesystem the agent inherits; a runtime that removes ambient authority by default addresses the actual failure mode
      • The open question for every such runtime is the same: which guarantees are enforced by the kernel/policy layer, and which are conventions the agent is merely asked to follow
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 442 pts / 201 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread is a boundary audit. Supporters welcome a vendor taking sandboxing, egress policy and credential isolation seriously, and compare the approach with micro-VMs, gVisor and plain containers, asking which enforcement layer OpenShell actually sits on. Sceptics press the guarantee question: a policy file is only as strong as its default, “safe and private” needs a threat model, and several commenters say they would not run untrusted agent code on it for personal use until the isolation claims are independently tested. A practical strand asks about operating cost — checkpointing, state storage, and what happens when an agent needs a legitimate exception — because fleet tooling lives or dies on exception handling, not on the demo policy.

    GNU Taler getting ready to operate in Germany: Taler Operations AG founded in Bochum to seek a BaFin e-money licence after GLS Bank stepped back

    GNU Taler is preparing to operate in Germany. After three years of work by the NGI Taler project to make Taler legally available in the EU as an everyday online payment system, a new company, Taler Operations AG, is being founded in Bochum to operate Taler in euro — first in Germany, then across the eurozone. The next step is applying to BaFin, Germany’s financial supervisor, for a licence as an e-money issuer; operations can only begin once BaFin grants authorisation, and no launch date is given. The reshuffle follows GLS Bank’s January 2026 decision not to take the e-money-issuer role it had originally planned, for reasons Taler says were unrelated to Taler itself; GLS stays in the consortium in a supporting role. Taler argues the dedicated operator is an advantage: the institution running Taler in euro will focus entirely on Taler rather than fitting it into a large bank’s portfolio and compliance processes.

    • The plan
      • Taler Operations AG, in formation in Bochum, subsidiary of Taler Systems SA (Luxembourg), will apply to BaFin as an electronic money institution
      • Sequence is licence first, launch second: no operations and no date until BaFin authorises
    • Why it matters
      • Taler’s design is cash-like for the payer — privacy for buyers, with merchants identified for tax — which is the opposite trade-off from account-based payments and from most crypto
      • A regulated EU operator is the difference between a published protocol and something a normal merchant can legally accept; this announcement is about crossing that line, not a new technical release
    Source: taler.net · Discuss: HN Discuss · 334 pts / 152 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingCommenters who know Taler explain it patiently to those who do not: the payer is anonymous the way cash is, the payee is not, which is why tax authorities can live with it and why “crypto” comparisons mislead. The licensing news is read as the real hurdle being attempted at last — an e-money licence is slow, expensive and exactly what a payment system needs before merchants will touch it. Sceptics ask who will accept it, note the chicken-and-egg problem against cards and instant bank transfers, and question spending limits and onboarding friction under EU rules. A smaller strand, mostly European, says the strategic context matters: a non-card, non-US payment rail in the eurozone has value beyond its first-year transaction volume.

    Ask HN: if the models are so good, why doesn’t OpenAI just start 100 companies? — thread says the bottleneck was never code

    The Ask HN poster puts it plainly: if frontier models are so good, why is selling tokens the primary business? Could OpenAI or Anthropic make more money by starting companies that leverage the models and building their own products, especially as token prices commodify and fall — indicating more money on the application side than the utility side? The question assumes model capability is the scarce input to a successful company. The thread, 307 comments, is a sustained rebuttal of that assumption from people who have run companies: ideas, distribution, trust, sales cycles, regulatory liability, support and timing are the scarce inputs, and a model improves mainly the one input — building the first version — that was already the cheapest part. It is an Ask HN with no source link; the post and its answers are the whole story.

    • The question
      • If models are the advantage, capturing application-layer margin directly should beat selling commodifying tokens — so why not?
      • Premise to test: that model access is a durable moat when every competitor can rent the same models
    • Why it matters
      • The answer generalises beyond OpenAI: it is the current best short explanation of why AI capability has not collapsed the startup landscape into the model labs
      • It also cuts the other way — labs are moving into applications, and platform-versus-customer conflict is now a real risk for startups built on rented models
    Source: Ask HN (thread only) · Discuss: HN Discuss · 276 pts / 307 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingFounders and operators dominate, and they agree more than they argue: starting companies is easy, starting companies that acquire customers, survive procurement, carry liability and retain users is the work, and none of that is token-shaped. Several note OpenAI and Anthropic already do versions of this — first-party applications, equity and partnership structures, and picking winners through distribution — without taking on 100 balance sheets of operational risk. The sharpest counterpoint is about incentives: a lab running competing applications poisons its own API market, the classic platform trap. A minority defend the premise, arguing labs are leaving money on the table in verticals where they do have distribution. Nobody credible in the thread claims code generation was the bottleneck.
