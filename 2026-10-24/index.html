    Local Wikipedia holds #1 for a second day: Wikipedia, but only for things a half-hour walk from you — now 1,434 points

    Local Wikipedia rebuilds Wikipedia around one location: only geocoded articles within about a half-hour walk are included. If fewer than 25 things are close, it takes the closest 25 within about 6 miles; if more than 500 qualify, only the closest 500 are included. You can start from a town, area, landmark or postcode, or use your browser location — the site says your location stays in your browser and shared links do not include it. Content is taken directly from Wikipedia and the site has no affiliation with Wikipedia. It was #2 yesterday morning at 693 points; by today it is #1 at 1,434, which is the rare HN pattern of a small, finished idea compounding instead of spiking and dying.

    • How it works
      • Geocoded Wikipedia pages only, filtered by walking distance; floor of 25 closest within ~6 miles, cap of 500 closest
      • Location stays in the browser; shared links do not carry your location
    • Why it matters
      • It inverts the usual feed: no algorithm, no engagement ranking — just what physically exists near you, with an article attached
      • A reminder that Wikipedia already holds a dense local layer most people never see because search starts from names, not places
    Source: edjefferson.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 1434 pts / 238 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe second-day thread stays warm and gets more specific about limits. Rural readers report the sparse end of the design: every suggestion is a one-sentence village entry.

    Kimi K3.1 Pro: a 3T-class open-weight model with a 1M-token window — Terminal-Bench 65.7, weights by Nov 4, cyber data deliberately removed

    Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro is a post-trained version of Kimi K3, described as its most capable 3-trillion-class open-weight model to date, with a 1-million-token context window; a Highspeed variant runs 2–3× faster. The vendor's own numbers put it at 65.7 on Terminal-Bench 4.0, ahead of GPT-6 Astra at 58.2 and narrowly behind Claude Opus 5.5 at 66.4, and at 92.7 on BenchCAD with tools. On its internal Hallu Bench Hard, the claim-level hallucination rate fell from 23.4% for K3 to 8.3%. The release is also a policy document: Moonshot says it identified and removed cyber-related data from both mid-training and RL stages for the open-weight release, cutting its internal xdev-bench-hard exploit score from 90.5% for an uncleaned checkpoint to 81.0%, and states it will not release open weights that extend the absolute frontier of cyber capability. It is live today on Kimi's apps and API; full weights are promised by November 4.

    • The numbers, vendor-reported
      • Terminal-Bench 4.0: 65.7 vs GPT-6 Astra 58.2 and Claude Opus 5.5 66.4; BenchCAD with tools: 92.7
      • Internal hallucination stress test: claim-level rate 23.4% (K3) down to 8.3%; API pricing posted at $2.00 / $10.00 per million input / output tokens
    • Why it matters
      • An open-weight model at or near the proprietary frontier changes the deployment maths: the capability can run on infrastructure the user controls, at posted token prices below the base K3 in China pricing cited in-thread
      • The cyber-data removal is the concrete part: a lab publishing both the uncleaned and cleaned scores (90.5 vs 81.0) shows capability control as a measurable training decision, not a slogan — and users who wanted the un-nerfed model will say so, below

    Canada's Bill C-22 is in the Senate: EFF says time is running out to stop mandated backdoors, a year of metadata retention, and US data sharing without a Canadian court

    The EFF warns that Bill C-22, Canada's Lawful Access Act, has passed the House of Commons and is now before the Senate. As the EFF and a CCLA / Citizen Lab analysis describe it, Part 2 would let the Minister of Public Safety order digital services — telecoms, messaging apps and more — to help with surveillance and to create a backdoor, so long as it does not create a 'systemic insecurity'; it would require services to retain metadata for up to a year, revealing who people communicate with, where they go and what services they use, even when they are not suspected of a crime; and it could expand sharing of Canadians' personal data with foreign law enforcement, including a negotiated cross-border agreement that would let US police obtain information about Canadian internet users without Canadian court oversight. CCLA and Citizen Lab call for withdrawing Part 2 entirely, not amending it, and CCLA is running a petition; some senators have already raised constitutional privacy concerns. This is an advocacy post by the EFF, and it presents the bill at its most dangerous reading — the mechanism descriptions above are specific and checkable against the bill text.

    • The mechanisms at issue
      • A ministerial order power to compel services to build surveillance capability / a backdoor, bounded only by a 'systemic insecurity' test
      • Metadata retention up to a year for people not suspected of any crime; cross-border sharing that can bypass Canadian court oversight
    • Why it matters
      • A backdoor that must not create systemic insecurity is a contradiction in engineering terms: any access mechanism built for one authorised party is an attack surface for every other party, which is the technical core of the opposition
      • This is the live legislative case, not a hypothetical — House passed, Senate stage, petition open — so the window the headline names is real and short
    Source: eff.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 451 pts / 233 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingCanadian commenters dominate, and the prevailing mood is resignation rather than debate: political apathy, a Senate that rarely kills bills, an MP crossing the floor, and a sense the government is already talking to companies about building the handover infrastructure because passage is assumed. The pro-bill argument present is the public-safety one — the public would rather be safe than free, and politicians are mirroring that. Technical readers attack the drafting itself: weasel-worded restrictions that do not actually bar compelling retention or production of communication content, gag orders with no eventual transparency mechanism, and rule-making latitude left to future regulation; one predicts major messaging services will simply block Canada if it passes unamended. Others widen the frame — reciprocal CLOUD-Act-style treaties with the US being negotiated by Canada and the EU at the same time as data-sovereignty talk, and India's version of such a law cited as the worked example of abuse. The actionable strand is short: sign the CCLA petition, join CCLA, talk to your MP.

    Dessa: a conversation model trained only on speech — no text, no pretrained model, full-duplex, trained on one GB200 NVL72 rack at Block

    Block's Dessa lab says it trained a conversation model from scratch on nothing but human speech: no text, no pretrained models. Dessa is full-duplex — it listens and talks at the same time, backchannels, interrupts, laughs mid-sentence rather than on cue, and gets louder when asked — and inference runs faster than real time, locally on a MacBook Pro M4 Max or better. The lab stresses what the text pipeline throws away: emotion, sarcasm, pauses and timing never survive transcription, so a model that never sees text has to learn language plus all of that from raw sound, at thousands of times more bytes per word than text. It also says Dessa learned from hundreds of times fewer words than an LLM needs, and was trained on a single NVIDIA GB200 NVL72. There is no product, API or weights announcement in the post: the team is offering early access to a small group of AI safety researchers. The demos on the page are live recordings, with subtitles added afterwards.

    • The claim
      • Speech in, speech out, learned only from recorded human conversation — language, facts, emotion and turn-taking all from sound
      • Trained on one GB200 NVL72; runs faster than real time on an M4 Max-class laptop
    • Why it matters
      • If language and usable knowledge can be learned from speech alone at a fraction of text-corpus scale, the text-first assumption behind current LLMs is a choice, not a law — that is a research result, whatever the demos are worth
      • The safety framing cuts both ways, as the thread notes: a model that hears distress is safer in one direction, and a voice this natural is a better scam instrument in the other
    Source: block.xyz · Discuss: HN Discuss · 422 pts / 158 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe thread splits cleanly between the research claim and the demo quality. Defenders who found it natural call it genuinely unsettling in a good way, and make the substantive point: voice carries emotional information text cannot, so speech-only training may be the route to models that actually perceive how something was said. The misuse strand is blunt: realistic conversational tics plus voice cloning is a better fraud call to a grandparent, and human-sounding support agents are a deception product, not a feature.

    The Real Kevin Bacon is Samuel L. Jackson: 2.09 average degrees on a 169,000-actor dataset — and Bacon isn't the centre on the older full-IMDb dataset either

    The Pudding's data piece, by Owen Lacey, re-runs Six Degrees of Kevin Bacon as a measurement instead of a party game. On its dataset — summarised in coverage as about 169,000 actors — the centre of Hollywood is Samuel L. Jackson: every other actor is on average 2.09 degrees of separation from him. That result is plausible on its face: Jackson is among the highest-grossing actors in history, has appeared in more than 200 films over roughly 50 years, and sits inside several cast-heavy franchises (Marvel, Star Wars, Kingsman) plus ensemble films like Pulp Fiction. The necessary caveat is in the thread, below: centrality is a dataset decision. The older Oracle of Bacon, computed over a fuller IMDb set, puts Eric Roberts first and Jackson fifth, so 'the real Kevin Bacon' depends on which films and actors you count. The Pudding piece is an interactive scrollytelling presentation; several readers report it looping or stalling on iOS Safari.

    • The result
      • Samuel L. Jackson: average 2.09 degrees to every other actor in the Pudding dataset; also the actor several readers guessed from his box-office-gross record
      • Presentation is tap-to-advance scrollytelling — praised for content, criticised for navigation and iOS Safari behaviour
    • Why it matters
      • A clean demonstration that a folk statistic survives contact with data only in spirit: almost everyone really is a few steps from everyone else, but the named centre is an artefact of dataset scope

    Twelve years of distributed SQLite: rqlite put Raft in front of SQLite in 2014 — consensus, then streaming, then merge, and the money went to the platforms

    Philip O'Toole, rqlite's author, writes the history of distributed SQLite from the start, because, he says, people now writing about the category tend to begin around 2021. In September 2014 he published rqlite — SQLite with a Raft log in front of it, replicating every write so losing a node does not lose the data — which he believes was the first open-source system to combine Raft and SQLite. The design decision that never changed: rqlite is a CP system, writes go through the leader and a partitioned minority refuses writes. The timeline that follows is the category forming: dqlite from Canonical in 2017 (a library you embed, where rqlite is a server you run), Litestream in 2021 (no consensus at all — stream the WAL to S3, because most applications need recovery, not a cluster), LiteFS, cr-sqlite's CRDT merge approach and libSQL/Turso in 2022, Cloudflare D1 the same year, and, he notes, Turso's acquisition by Supabase in 2026. His summary of the design space: three corners, appearing in order — consensus, streaming, merge — each correct for a different question.

    • The history
      • 2014 rqlite (Raft + SQLite, CP, leader writes); 2017 dqlite; 2021 Litestream (WAL to S3) reframed the problem as recovery, not clustering
      • 2022 platforms: D1, LiteFS, Turso/libSQL, cr-sqlite — replicated SQLite becomes a thing you buy, and in 2026 Supabase acquires Turso, per the post
    • Why it matters
      • The author's closing point is the measured one: the core idea was open source and free for eight years before anyone sold it, and the projects that raised capital or were acquired are the ones that turned it into a service with a bill
      • For anyone choosing today, the three-corner frame is the useful takeaway: do you need no data loss on node failure (consensus), cheap recovery (streaming), or many writers (merge)? Picking the wrong corner is most distributed-SQLite regret
    Source: philipotoole.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 263 pts / 41 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingA small, practitioner thread. Production users vouch for rqlite directly — one runs it as the control-plane database for an open-source observability product and praises the maintainer by name — while Litestream users explain why they have not switched: single-node SQLite plus WAL streaming is safe enough when some data loss in the extreme case is acceptable, and a cluster is much slower than one node. The critical strand is also present: the D1 section is read as sudden snark, the ending as defensive about priority, a reader asks the Jepsen question — has it been Jepsen-tested, because if not, problems are likely lurking — and a D1-in-production user passes on a D1 developer’s own advice that Durable Objects storage is usually the better choice because D1 is itself a singleton Durable Object with a SQL wrapper. A forward-looking minority asks whether agents change the calculus: Postgres is now easy to operate with agent help and has the fuller SQL, so the case for distributed SQLite has to be made on locality and simplicity, not on avoiding a database server.
