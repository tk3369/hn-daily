    OpenAI claims three discoveries: local smoothing and Kakeya in all dimensions, ETH false, and QCD existence

    OpenAI says its internal models produced three results — a proof of Sogge’s local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which it says also resolves the Kakeya and Stein restriction conjectures; a refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis; and a rigorous existence proof for interacting four-dimensional quantum field theories including QCD. None is peer-reviewed yet. The release process is the experiment: outside experts act as unpaid, NDA-free “shepherds,” the math and computer science papers ship public with Lean formalizations, and the physics group — led by Nobel laureate David Gross — is holding its materials back to write them up itself.

    • Why it matters

    “The Exponential Time Hypothesis Is False” [PDF]: a subexponential exact counter for k-SAT, with Lean proof

    This is the standalone paper behind story 2’s computer science claim, posted separately and voted up on its own: a deterministic algorithm that exactly counts the satisfying assignments of an arbitrary k-CNF formula in superpolynomial but subexponential time — stated in summaries of the Lean formalization as 2^O(n·(log log n)²/√log n) with polynomial space. ETH, used in hundreds of papers to prove lower bounds across graph theory, biology, databases and geometry, says 3-SAT cannot be solved that fast. The paper itself is careful about scope: bounds of the form 2^o(n) do not transfer under general polynomial-time reductions, so this does not by itself give subexponential algorithms for all NP problems.

    • The result
      • Exact counting for fixed-width k-CNF in subexponential time refutes ETH via 3-SAT; Lean verification accompanies the natural-language proof on GitHub (openai/math)
      • Shepherds for the CS collaboration: Impagliazzo, Paturi and Williams — established complexity theorists, unpaid and independent
    • Why it matters
      • If it holds, every conditional lower bound that assumed ETH needs re-examination — the assumption was load-bearing across multiple fields
      • It is not P=NP: subexponential is still far from polynomial, and the authors flag the reduction-transfer limit in the paper itself
    Source: github.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 690 pts / 256 comments

    What HN commenters are saying“This seems huge, like not quite P=NP but in the same league — am I missing something?” gets a flat “No. It’s huge.” Then the thread does the careful work: it is not P=NP, and the claim that NP is therefore subexponential is corrected in detail — reductions to 3-SAT can blow up variables, and the paper’s own note says 2^o(n) bounds do not survive general polynomial reductions. The counterargument that survives is narrower and still large: anything linearly reducible to k-SAT is now subexponential, linear reductions just became much more important, and the technique may apply to other problems for different reasons. Scepticism is about scope, not dismissal.

    Oldest GUI app that still runs on Windows 11: CDPLAYER.EXE linked 16 Nov 1992, almost 34 years old

    The hunt rules out 16-bit Windows programs — 64-bit Windows has no NTVDM — so the search is early NT. FreeCell from Win32s, dated 22 Feb 1993 and fully playable, was the previous answer. Digging through NT 3.1 pre-release builds on the Internet Archive found older: CDPLAYER.EXE from Build 347, linked 16 November 1992. Early builds mostly fail — draft PE formats and a cdecl-vs-stdcall calling-convention change crash them — but CD Player avoids the bundled C runtime and uses the system crtdll.dll, which Windows 11 still ships in SysWOW64. It runs; with a small cdemu.dll emulating a CD-ROM drive, it actually plays. Cheating section included: via otvdm, Reversi from Windows 1.01 (15 Nov 1985) also runs.

    • The result
      • Unmodified winner: CDPLAYER.EXE, 16 Nov 1992, almost 34 years old, running on current Windows 11; earlier answer was FreeCell, 22 Feb 1993
      • Why it survives: fixed stdcall convention by that build, no bundled runtime — it links the system crtdll.dll that Windows still ships
    • Why it matters
      • A measured data point on backward compatibility as a platform strategy: a 1992 binary runs because Microsoft kept the ABI and the DLL
      • The contrast in-thread is Linux, where commenters note even cross-distro binaries from this decade are unreliable without containers
    Source: blog.enlyth.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 479 pts / 282 comments

    Solar overtakes nuclear: 1% of world power a decade ago, ~10% in 2026, and “offensively cheap”

    BBC Future, analysing Ember data, reports solar generation quietly overtook nuclear in 2025 and, over the 12 months to September 2026, produced 13% more electricity than all the world’s nuclear plants. A decade ago solar was 1% of global power; in 2026 it will almost certainly reach 10%. In 2025 solar met three-quarters of the growth in world electricity demand — the fastest-growing source of new electricity in history. Oxford’s Jan Rosenow names the forecasting failure directly: mainstream outlooks assumed linear growth and slowly falling costs, and got both wrong, year after year. Drivers in the piece include Chinese manufacturing scale pushing module prices to record lows, and batteries largely solving the daily shift of midday sun into the evening; seasonal storage remains hard.

    • The numbers
      • Ember via BBC: solar passed nuclear in 2025; +13% over nuclear in the 12 months to Sep 2026; ~1% of world power a decade ago to ~10% in 2026
      • Pakistan as the speed case, per Rosenow: solar went from its fifth-largest electricity source in 2023 to its largest in 2025
    • Why it matters
      • Generation share, not capacity announcements: solar is now producing more electricity than a technology that has been a mainstay since the late 1970s
      • Forecasting lesson generalises: exponential cost-and-deployment curves beat linear institutional projections — the experts quoted say so about their own field
    Source: bbc.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 435 pts / 612 comments

    Blaise Pascal invented fixed-route public transport: Paris, 1662, five sols, every 7.5–15 minutes (2024)

    Jarrett Walker’s Human Transit essay argues Pascal’s last project was not theology but transit: the carrosses à cinq sols, launched 18 March 1662 and network-complete by July, weeks before his death at 39. Eight-passenger horse-drawn carriages ran fixed, signed routes at 7.5–15 minute frequencies — frequent enough to just go out and wait — across multiple connecting lines including an orbital. Walker’s point is the invention is the constraint: carriages could go anywhere, and the usefulness came from choosing not to, following paths people could remember and plan around. The network design already shows modern principles — lines spaced apart, perpendicular crossings to maximise connections, straight cross-city routes, and an orbital loop. Its main failure was overcrowding.

    • The system
      • Paris 1662: fixed routes with endpoint signs, fixed five-sol fare, 7.5–15 min headways, multiple lines forming a network, first line 18 March, complete by July
      • Source is a Dec 2024 essay resurfacing today; Walker credits Taras Grescoe’s reporting and notes Wikipedia’s “invented the bus line” is too narrow
    • Why it matters
      • Fixed-route service is an information invention, not a vehicle invention: predictability is what lets strangers plan trips without negotiation
      • Design principles visible in a 1662 hand-drawn network — spacing, perpendicularity, orbitals — are the same ones transit planners still teach
    Source: humantransit.org · Discuss: HN Discuss · 302 pts / 157 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe priority claim gets a well-sourced challenge: the Dutch trekschuit, from 1632, ran fixed passenger routes on canals dug straight for the purpose and carried 30 passengers — land-adjacent, scheduled and cheap, with an extensive network by 1700. Others debate whether ancient ferries and Roman scheduled sailings count, noting wind and tide made true scheduling unreliable before steam. A second thread argues about the century of maximum change — 1865–1965, extended to 1969 for the Moon landing — via grandparents who drove horse wagons and later lived with personal computers. The forward-looking comment asks the modern version of Pascal’s question: with anonymised travel data and existing fleets, how much efficiency is current route design leaving on the table?

    Roblox crawler downloaded 145.5 GB from Open3DLab sites — 1,027 files, 712 projects, adult content included, no reply

    Open3DLab says a crawler identifying as trellis2-data-sourcing/1.0 (3D foundation model research, with a Roblox contact address) read the main page of 18,926 projects — practically the entire catalogue across Open3DLab, SFMLab and SmutBase — in 26,429 requests over 34 hours from a single AWS address on 15–16 October, then downloaded complete copies of 1,027 files from 712 projects, 145.5 GB in all. It retrieved their robots.txt and ignored it, and ignored terms forbidding scraping; 423 of the 712 projects are licensed non-commercial only. Included: 364 files (54.8 GB) from the adults-only SmutBase. The sites blocked it on 18 October, emailed the user-agent address twice, and report no reply from Roblox. Their stated assumption — labelled speculation in the post — is AI training data for Roblox’s Cube 3D foundation model work.

    • The numbers
      • 26,429 requests / 34 hours / one AWS address; 1,027 files, 145.5 GB, 712 projects; free download-server caps were the main limit on how much was taken
      • Licence exposure: 423 of 712 projects non-commercial only; 345 projects (62.6 GB) adult content when SFMLab adult projects are included
    • Why it matters
      • Robots.txt and terms are norms, not enforcement: a crawler that identifies itself honestly can still take everything before a small site notices
      • Non-response is part of the story — two emails, no reply, and scraping attempts resumed on 19–20 October against the blocks
    Source: open3dlab.com · Discuss: HN Discuss · 294 pts / 183 comments

    What HN commenters are sayingThe practical defence thread dominates: block all AWS, DigitalOcean and Azure traffic unless you have a valid reason to serve datacentres, since major providers publish their IP ranges. Counterarguments arrive fast — most bot traffic now comes from residential proxies (one commenter’s traffic this week is mostly residential IPs all claiming the same Chrome 155 on Linux, possibly from “superbox” TV-streamer devices), and blocking cloud ranges locks out office and VDI users whose egress is Azure or GCP, which one business learned after banning datacentre IPs and having to remove the rule. Cloudflare Turnstile is offered as the middle path. The Roblox conduct itself draws little defence in the captured comments; the argument is about what a small site can actually do.
