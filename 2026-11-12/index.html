Tim Peters, author of Timsort and the Zen of Python, has died

Tim Peters, a Python core developer since the 1990s, has died. He wrote Timsort, the sorting algorithm now used in Python, Java and Rust, and the Zen of Python — “import this” — that still ships with every Python install. The community is marking the loss of one of its founding voices.

Source HN Discuss 1319 pts · 157 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Tributes from people who knew him from the Python Usenet group and bug tracker in the 90s, and from those who only knew his work: Timsort and the Zen. Several hope newer generations keep the Zen alive in the agentic era, though one commenter admits that hope feels shakier by the day.

AWS emulator: run AWS services locally

AWS released an open-source local emulator for its services, built largely by AI agents iterating on the codebase. The aim: test against S3, Lambda and friends without touching the cloud. The thread wants proof it’s a maintained product, not a one-off demo.

Source HN Discuss 911 pts · 251 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Skepticism dominates — is this a real product AWS will maintain, or a marketing exercise in agent-built code? An AWS voice in the thread says it’s a real product they plan to maintain. Others point to LocalStack, Floci and Testcontainers as the incumbents, and wonder aloud about the token spend per iteration.

United Airlines bans AI agents from booking flights

United Airlines says AI agents may not book flights on behalf of travelers, joining the pushback against agentic booking. United’s argument: it doesn’t want another powerful intermediary between it and passengers, after search, metasearch and OTAs. The ban is a test case for whether airlines can keep agents out.

Source HN Discuss 617 pts · 636 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The quoted line — United doesn’t want “yet another, and potentially even more powerful, intermediary” — draws the obvious reply: focus on service and price instead. Others push back: the market has decided, price beats everything, and airlines that tried service-first went bankrupt. Alaska gets a mixed report card.

A false smoke alarm triggered the water-based fire suppression in Jane Street’s only New York datacenter — nearly flooding the room. The author, who lived minutes away, got the 3am call. It’s a war story about single points of failure, and about the cost of telling coworkers you live close to the office.

Source HN Discuss 534 pts · 256 comments

7

93% of paint splatters are valid Perl programs (2019)

A 2019 SIGBOVIK paper found 93% of randomly generated paint splatters parse as valid Perl programs, thanks mostly to Perl’s unquoted-string quirk — a bare word like ME evaluates as the string “ME”. It’s a joke paper with a real point about how permissive Perl’s parser is.

Source HN Discuss 512 pts · 66 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Delight at the paper itself — one commenter flags “Figure − 3. − 3 .” as a good easter egg — plus the technical explanation: unquoted strings do most of the work. A side thread notes older PHP had the same feature, deprecated in PHP 7 and gone in 8.

Jane Street publishes a Socratic Tutor agent skill

Jane Street open-sourced a Socratic Tutor skill for coding agents: instead of answering, the agent asks questions that lead you to the answer. It’s a teaching pattern, not a new idea — dozens of similar skills already exist — but Jane Street’s version is the most elaborate of the bunch.

Source HN Discuss 472 pts · 107 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Novelty is the debate: a GitHub search returns 92 repositories for “socratic tutor skill,” and several commenters built their own in 2024. Defenders say Jane Street’s is genuinely more complex than the lookalikes, and Socratic questioning is a well-known technique nobody owns.

10

Lock your GitHub Actions: pin by commit, verify with lockfiles

A guide to locking down GitHub Actions: pin actions by commit hash instead of a movable tag, and use Actions lockfiles so an impostor commit can’t slip in. The debate is about who you trust — the repo owner not to move a tag, or the person who merged the hash.

Source HN Discuss 306 pts · 60 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The core trade-off, argued both ways: hash-pinning trusts whoever merged the commit to have checked it’s a real release, not some fork’s commit — and any fork is the same repo on GitHub. Lockfiles are the agreed fix: they won’t lock an impostor commit. Some only use actions they can review in under an hour.
