Anthropic researcher claims a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis

Levent Alpöge, working with an internal Anthropic AI model, generated a Lean-only proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. Terence Tao says the strategy appears to use the full power of the Langlands program and modern arithmetic geometry. Nothing is verified yet: the formalization is enormous, and digestion will take weeks at best.

Source HN Discuss 2756 pts · 1932 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The thread is a mix of awe and disciplined skepticism. The top comments try to explain what GRH even says — one uses a grid of 1s to show how primes fall out of the pattern — while others note the Lean formalization is public for anyone to check. The recurring caution: prolific mathematicians have claimed big results before and been wrong, and 'insane if true' is not a verification. A side thread asks why a maths story with no news hook is on the front page at all; the answer is that the Lean proof is available to read now.

A second, all-human proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis surfaces the same day

Hours after the Anthropic announcement, Ravi Vakil rushed out Alessandro Masullo's fully human GRH proof. Brian Conrad had been checking it for months and it looked good so far, but the plan had been a year of polishing first. Within about eight hours, mathematicians reported problems in the draft, found with AI assistance. It stays unproven until the community resolves them.

Source HN Discuss 2255 pts · 1066 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The human story dominates: Masullo worked on the problem for 12 years, and commenters imagine the feeling of seeing the Anthropic announcement land first. The strategy debate is sharp — keep it tight with a few trusted reviewers, or publish fast and claim a tie? Several point out the cruel asymmetry: formalizing a proof yourself costs millions in compute, while your rival's lab can throw models at it. Others note the irony that AI tools are now the ones finding holes in the human proof.

Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini: a looped 26B model that runs on a laptop

Inkling 2 Mini is a mixture-of-experts model — 26.4B total parameters, 3.4B active — that runs its middle layers three times per token, reasoning longer without writing its reasoning down. It leads every open-weights model under 100B parameters on DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%), and it is sized to run locally.

Source HN Discuss 1146 pts · 217 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Local-model users are the loudest fans: a model this capable at this size is 'bonkers good if the benchmarks hold up in the real world.' The looped-transformer design draws the technical interest — more reasoning per token without a longer chain of thought — and one commenter reports grafting reasoning into a 6.5M-parameter model of their own. The sizing was explicitly informed by what the local-running community asks for most.

An essay argues the architect title landed on developers by default: as AI writes more of the code, the job is now design decisions, data models, and pushing back on requirements — whether or not anyone announced the promotion. The thread mostly agrees the work was always the job; the title just caught up, and the hands-on-keyboard part was never the core of it.

Source HN Discuss 503 pts · 281 comments

Mostly agreement, with the pushback coming from people who say every healthy team already expected this: architects were often figureheads, and the dev picking up the ticket made the design calls anyway. The sharper thread is about AI's role — several say the software was never the core part of the job; finding people with interesting problems and solving them was. 'Architects are a bad meme, along the same lines as scrum masters,' one commenter adds, to some applause.

Tao confirms three AI-assisted breakthroughs: Euler, Navier-Stokes, and GRH

Terence Tao reports that Alpöge, Tristan Buckmaster and Matei Coiculescu produced Lean-formalized proofs of finite-time blowup for the unforced Euler and unforced Navier-Stokes equations in three dimensions — plus Alpöge's Lean-only GRH proof. All three used heavy AI assistance. The Navier-Stokes paper is technically readable; a professionally written version still needs work.

Source HN Discuss 489 pts · 210 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The thread wrestles with what AI can and cannot do yet. The consensus guess: models can't build new theory, but their brute-force verification turns out to be surprisingly powerful — and theory-building may follow. Several expect the next two weeks of digestion to settle whether the GRH proof holds, and whether the concurrent human announcement muddies the story. One commenter quotes a mathematician's take that models work at such a high level of abstraction they don't need theory; theory is for us, so we can understand what they did.

Language models can now rewrite their own weights — no gradients, no training

An open 27B model, given its own weights and graded answers, diagnoses its failures and edits its weights by reasoning alone — no gradients, no training run. It gained 6.6 points on unseen problems, improved across eight consecutive sessions, and repaired a damaged copy of itself to over 80% of original accuracy without ever seeing the original weights.

Source HN Discuss 443 pts · 128 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The thread is careful about the headline: the only fix the model made was shortening its reasoning traces to avoid hitting the token limit — an obvious flaw, and the same pattern as earlier weight-space programming work. Still, the mechanism impresses: the agent finds a common error category, computes the aggregated difference between error and non-error states, and pushes weights along it — gradient descent approximated without gradients. The open question is whether this scales past trivial flaws.

Global markets slumped as the AI-spending payback question deepened: the Nasdaq gave back all of its 2026 gains, and Anthropic fell on its second day of trading after its blockbuster IPO. The sell-off is broad, not one company's story — the question now is whether AI capex ever pays for itself.

Source HN Discuss 400 pts · 716 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The biggest thread is economics, not markets: if commodity AI automates a $1B industry, does GDP fall? The careful answer is no — GDP counts output, not headcount — but the follow-up lands harder: who buys anything when most of the country is unemployed? Several argue the only number worth optimizing is happiness, not GDP, and that the middle class gets forgotten when cost-cutting is the whole strategy.

The thread splits: frame the gap as an AI-powered solo venture with a product to show, or skip the spin entirely and be honest — every hiring manager has now seen every version of the embellished story, and LLMs make the claims cheap to check.

Ask HN — no external source HN Discuss 339 pts · 343 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The advice fight is the thread. One camp says the gap needs a story: an AI-powered SaaS built solo, lessons learned, traction attempted. It exists because Reznor spent decades encouraging taping and sharing — handing out HD camcorders to fans in the Year Zero era instead of cracking down on bootlegs — and the live show itself is famously theatrical, with hidden audience cameras streamed on stage.

Source HN Discuss 303 pts · 80 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Affectionate and a little awestruck. Long-time fans praise the sound quality and the multimedia staging — in one show, hidden cameras pointed at the audience were streamed live on stage. The verdict is unanimous: the output has only improved with time.
