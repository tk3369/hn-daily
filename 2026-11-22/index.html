The human GRH proof is in trouble; the Lean-checked Riemann proof is certified

Ravi Vakil rushed out Alessandro Masullo's fully human proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis after Anthropic's AI-assisted claim, with Brian Conrad's months of vetting behind it. Within about eight hours, mathematicians reported problems in the draft, found with AI assistance — it stays unproven until the community resolves them. The Lean-checked Riemann proof was certified instead (story 3).

Source HN Discuss 2438 pts · 1188 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The thread is mostly about the race itself. Masullo worked on the problem for 12 years, and commenters sympathize with the position: publish a hurried draft and claim a timestamped tie, or keep it with trusted reviewers and risk an AI lab scooping you. The asymmetry draws the most heat — formalizing a proof at this scale costs millions, so a lone mathematician can't match a lab's compute, yet AI tools are now the ones finding the holes in his draft. A few compare it to search engines: knowledge becoming a commodity felt demoralizing at first, then people found new roles.

A pill that shrinks pancreatic tumours — 'biggest breakthrough in decades'

Daraxonrasib, a daily pill, made cancer undetectable in 1 in 12 patients in a 61-person trial, and tumours stopped growing in nearly all of them. A year after starting treatment 76.6% were alive, against 40–45% typically surviving a year on chemotherapy alone. Pancreatic cancer has barely moved in decades — the first targeted pill to show this kind of signal, though the trial is small and the patients late-stage.

Source HN Discuss 1172 pts · 248 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Cautious optimism with the caveats up front. The survival gain — roughly 13 months versus 6 — looks modest until you remember trials enroll very late-stage patients who have exhausted other options, and for them doubling remaining time is everything; one commenter writes about a friend facing exactly that arithmetic. Others note this is a different approach from the recent virus-injection results, and that chemo and radiation keep improving incrementally too — two promising pancreatic approaches in one month is itself the news.

The Riemann Hypothesis is now Lean-verified — 17 million lines, independently checked

The Lean and Mathlib communities certified Levent Alpöge's Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis: the 17-million-line library compiles, proves exactly Mathlib's statement of RH, and the raw proof export was checked by Lean's kernel plus independent checkers in other languages — including con-leche and con-ron, which carry formal proofs of their own consistency. If it holds, it closes a 167-year-old problem.

Source HN Discuss 1124 pts · 935 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Near-consensus that this is solid. The point commenters keep repeating: the statement proved is taken directly from Mathlib, long vetted, and the proof was checked by multiple independent kernels in different languages, including checkers with formal proofs of their own consistency — so a bug would have to hit every checker at once. The remaining questions are procedural: how many independent checkers are enough before calling it 100%, and what the formalization means for the Masullo claim running in parallel.

Toyota, Nissan and Honda will standardize parts to cut costs

Japan's three largest automakers will standardize parts — starting with common steel quality criteria, shared plastics protocols, and a universal wiring-harness color code — to cut production costs and compete with China's automakers. The JAMA announcement frames it as cost-cutting, but the supply-chain angle matters too: after years of shocks, standardizing basics keeps bottlenecks from stranding production.

Source HN Discuss 798 pts · 473 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The thread splits on motive. One camp reads it as cost competition: BYD makes 75–85% of parts in-house, so the Japanese makers are closing a structural gap. The other says it's supply-chain resilience — Fukushima taught Toyota to stockpile parts, and recent geopolitical shocks pushed JAMA to standardize basics so bottlenecks don't strand production. Most agree it's both, and a few hope the trend spreads to more automakers.

The Army has to blow up 10,000 pounds of lead azide — in place

The Army plans to detonate 68 drums — 9,500 to 10,000 pounds — of lead azide stored for about 20 years at an Iowa ammunition plant, after it recrystallized into a form too sensitive to move. The EPA issued an emergency permit for in-place detonation; the event was postponed to at least Monday by legal action. Derek Lowe explains why every alternative is worse — and why lead dust drifting 20–30 miles is the price.

Source HN Discuss 652 pts · 405 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The tone is grim respect from people who have handled primaries. Degraded lead azide can detonate from a loud noise or the flex of a container, so several commenters argue the blame belongs with whoever let an enormous stockpile sit uninspected for 20 years, not with whoever has to deal with it now. The practical consensus: don't move the drums — the only safe path is the controlled detonation the Army is planning, with monitors across eastern Iowa watching the fallout.

His monitor was eating 75% of his download speed

A 2.5 Gbps symmetric line delivered only 600 Mbps down (upload stayed at 2.4 Gbps) when the Ethernet adapter ran through an Apple Studio Display's USB hub. The culprit wasn't bandwidth — SSDs through the same hub were fine — but the adapter chip's small buffers: not enough packets in flight to fill the pipe. Plugged straight into the laptop, full speed returned.

Source HN Discuss 632 pts · 294 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The debugging story lands well, but the thread's energy goes to the root-cause debate: it's a latency and buffer problem, not bandwidth — the adapter chip can't keep enough packets in flight through the monitor's hub, which is why SSDs through the same hub are unaffected. From there it drifts into a long side discussion of io_uring and iowait accounting, with kernel folks arguing the metric itself is broken. The practical takeaway survives the drift: test your speed through the hub, not just at the laptop.

Inkling-2-Mini squeezes into 5.4 GB of memory

A quantization project fits Inkling-2-Mini into 5.4 GB using hashed block-vector trellis codes decoded by custom Metal kernels, with a GGUF route for llama.cpp in the repo. For now the released files are MX/Apple Silicon only. The thread's caution: fitting a model in tiny RAM is easy to over-claim — check the quality, not just the footprint.

Source HN Discuss 507 pts · 127 comments
What HN commenters are saying

Skepticism is the headline. 'Don't get too excited about claims of fitting large models into tiny RAM' — there is always a tradeoff, and KL-divergence numbers are easy to benchmaxx. The useful detail: released files are MX/Apple Silicon only for now, with a GGUF route intended for llama.cpp but not yet the way most people would run it. The counterpoint from a retired tinkerer: if you have time to play, try it; if you have work to do, stick to well-vetted setups and spend your time at the application layer.

87% of Anthropic's power will come from gas, 11% from coal, in 2028

A Better Data Center Project case study estimates Anthropic's compute will emit 32 million metric tons of CO2 in 2028, with 87% of its power from gas and 11% from coal — no direct US renewable commitments disclosed. The wider finding: 177 GW of planned 'bring your own power' gas capacity for data centers, enough to power 65% of US homes. The thread splits between regulation and whataboutism.

Source HN Discuss 325 pts · 437 comments
What HN commenters are saying

The thread divides fast. One side says this is exactly why regulation exists: externalities land on communities far from the shareholders, and a purely financial accounting ignores them. The other side goes straight to whataboutism — China burns more coal than the US and Europe combined — and gets the counter that Chinese projects are mostly remote, renewable-heavy greenfield sites, while US data centers land near homes with noise and dust. Underneath the argument, most agree the numbers are the story: 87% gas, 11% coal, and no renewable commitments disclosed.
